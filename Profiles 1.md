COUNTRY: Afghanistan
TITLE: Afghanistan — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/afghanistan
META DESCRIPTION: Afghanistan is one of the world's most dangerous countries for Christians. Under Taliban rule since 2021, Christian practice is secret and conversion from Islam can carry a death sentence.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Afghanistan, Central Asia, Taliban, Islamic Extremism, WWL Top 25, Apostasy, Secret Believers
PERSECUTION TIER: Extreme
WWL RANKING: #11 (Open Doors 2026)

## Overview

Afghanistan is one of the hardest places on earth to follow Christ. The Taliban have governed as an Islamic Emirate since August 2021. Conversion from Islam is treated as apostasy, and apostasy can be punished by death. Afghan Christians worship in secret. The country has no public church, no Christian institution, and no legal path for a Muslim to change faith. Open Doors ranked Afghanistan 11th on the 2026 World Watch List with a persecution score of 85. The drop from the top three in earlier years reflects how few incidents can now be verified, and not any improvement on the ground.

## Historical Roots of Christianity

Silk Road trade routes crossed this land long before Islam arrived. Nestorian Christians of the Church of the East planted communities along those routes by the third and fourth centuries. Evidence points to Christian presence in ancient Herat, Bagram, and the wider Khorasan region. By the fifth and sixth centuries there were organized Nestorian congregations under Sassanid Persian rule, with bishops and local leaders.

These communities stayed small. They were still real. The Arab Islamic conquest of the seventh century began a slow erasure. Two centuries of Islamic rule, conversion pressure, and social change wore the churches down. By the tenth century Nestorian Christianity had largely vanished from the region. The Mongol invasions of the thirteenth century and the campaigns of Timur in the fourteenth finished the work. By the Timurid period there was no organized Christian community anywhere in what became Afghanistan.

Western missionaries made limited contact during the British colonial period in the nineteenth century. They were never permitted to preach to Muslims. Afghanistan entered the twentieth century as one of the most uniformly Islamic societies on earth.

## The Soviet Era and Civil War, 1979 to 2001

The Soviet invasion of December 1979 opened a decade of catastrophic violence, followed by civil war. Small numbers of foreign aid workers, many of them Christian, worked in the country under strict agreements to avoid religious activity with Afghans. The mujahideen who drove the Soviets out were hard Islamists. Any Afghan believer in this period lived in total concealment.

The first Taliban government of 1996 to 2001 wrote into law what had long been true in practice. Afghanistan would tolerate no Christianity among its own people. Foreign relief workers kept operating at real risk, and several were arrested on charges of proselytizing. Apostasy meant death, and the Taliban said plainly that they intended to enforce it.

## The Post-9/11 Decade, 2001 to 2021

A narrow window opened after the U.S.-led invasion in October 2001. Western NGOs worked more freely. Some Afghan Christians who had come to faith abroad returned home. Small house church networks formed, mostly in Kabul. The 2004 constitution promised religious freedom for non-Muslims while requiring every law to conform to Islamic principles, and apostasy stayed a capital offense in prevailing legal opinion.

Several cases made the limits clear. Abdul Rahman converted while working for an international aid agency and was arrested on apostasy charges in 2006. International pressure won his release and emigration, and the case showed that Afghan law would not protect converts. Taliban fighters took twenty-three South Korean missionaries hostage in 2007 and killed two of them. The last public church building, a chapel on the grounds of a foreign compound, closed in 2010.

## Taliban Return and Current Crisis

The Taliban returned in August 2021 and the openings of the previous two decades closed within weeks. Known believers fled or disappeared. Underground networks in Kabul went dark. Converts holding foreign passports were evacuated. Those without an exit route were left in acute danger.

Open Doors, International Christian Concern, and Voice of the Martyrs all describe the same picture. Discovery can mean death. Family members who report a convert are treated as having defended the family's honor. In some districts the Taliban have searched house to house for converts. Phones and social media accounts are monitored for any sign of Christian contact. Converts are sometimes committed to psychiatric hospitals on the reasoning that no sane person would leave Islam.

There is no legal remedy, no international monitoring inside the country, and no open church anywhere in Afghanistan.

## Afghan Christians in the Diaspora

Afghan believers who flee to neighboring countries trade one danger for another. Refugee populations in Pakistan and Iran are overwhelmingly Muslim, and converts face harassment from fellow Afghans outside their homeland. In Pakistan, Afghan Christian refugees have been attacked in camps and swept up in mass deportation drives.

Larger numbers resettled in Germany, the United Kingdom, the United States, and Australia after the 2021 collapse. These diaspora communities keep contact with believers inside the country, fund underground work, and carry the advocacy load internationally.

## The Church Today

The Afghan church is underground and invisible. Open Doors describes the Christian population only as "thousands." Other advocacy estimates run from 10,000 to 15,000. No figure can be verified. Most believers meet in twos and threes. Larger gatherings are treated as too dangerous to attempt. Contact runs through encrypted messaging. Audio Scripture in Dari and Pashto circulates through secure channels, and satellite broadcasters such as SAT-7 reach some households by television.

The country has no seminary, no church building, no legal Christian broadcaster, and no Christian school.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Extreme (96/100), **Rank:** #1 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** thousands, unverifiable, all secret believers
- **Primary Threat:** Taliban governance, apostasy punishment, clan and family enforcement
- **Legal Status:** No public worship. Conversion from Islam treated as a capital offense
- **Churches:** None. The last public church closed in 2010
- **Bible:** Import illegal, possession dangerous
- **Foreign Missionaries:** Banned

COUNTRY: Albania
TITLE: Albania — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/albania
META DESCRIPTION: Albania was declared the world's first atheist state in 1967. Today it has religious freedom, and its Christian community still carries the damage of decades of communist suppression.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Albania, Southeast Europe, Communism, Religious Freedom, Eastern Orthodox, Balkans
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Albania holds an unwanted distinction. For more than two decades it was the only officially atheist state in the world. Enver Hoxha banned religion outright in 1967, closed or demolished every place of worship, and jailed or executed clergy. Albania today is legally free and socially tolerant. The damage from that period still shapes the church. Recovery has been slow, congregations are small, and religious knowledge across the population remains thin.

## Historical Roots of Christianity

Christianity reached Roman Illyricum very early. Paul wrote in Romans 15:19 that he had preached as far as Illyricum, and tradition places the faith on Albanian soil in the first century. Organized church structures grew under Byzantine rule. Albania then settled into a division that still holds, with Eastern Orthodoxy in the south and Roman Catholicism in the north.

Ottoman conquest in the fifteenth century brought Islam, which spread over the following three centuries. Albanian national identity never attached itself to one faith. The independence movement of the nineteenth century was deliberately multi-confessional, and Pashko Vasa famously wrote that the religion of Albanians was Albanianism.

## Communist Suppression, 1967 to 1990

Hoxha declared Albania an atheist state in 1967. The policy was literal. All 2,169 religious buildings in the country were closed, demolished, or converted into sports halls, warehouses, and cultural centers. Religious practice of any kind became a crime. Clergy went to prison. Some who kept practicing in secret were executed or sentenced to decades of forced labor.

Catholic clergy suffered worst. Archbishop Vinçens Prennushi died in prison in 1949. Dozens of priests were executed in the late 1940s and early 1950s. The Catholic Church went entirely underground. By the time the regime fell in 1990, almost no clergy were left. Most had died, fled, or survived in hiding.

## Post-Communist Recovery and Current Status

Legal religious freedom returned in 1990 and 1991. Rebuilding from zero took decades. Archbishop Anastasios Yannoulatos, a Greek scholar who arrived in 1992, led the restoration of the Orthodox Church of Albania. Catholic dioceses reorganized. Protestant and evangelical missions entered through the 1990s and remain small.

The 2023 census recorded about 45 percent Muslim, 8 percent Catholic, and 7 percent Orthodox, with roughly a quarter of respondents declining to answer or reporting no religion. Those figures sit well below earlier estimates and show how much the atheist period erased. The constitution guarantees religious freedom and the government generally respects it. Relations between communities are cooperative, and Albanians point with justified pride to the sheltering of Jews during the Holocaust.

Albania is not a persecution country by any standard measure. Its challenge is memory rather than repression.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (5/100), **Rank:** #152 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** roughly 15 to 20 percent by the 2023 census, Orthodox and Catholic combined
- **Primary Historical Threat:** Communist atheist suppression, 1967 to 1990
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom, no systematic persecution
- **Notable:** Albania sheltered Jews during the Holocaust regardless of faith, a record unmatched in occupied Europe

COUNTRY: Algeria
TITLE: Algeria — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/algeria
META DESCRIPTION: Algeria was once the heartland of North African Christianity. Today it is 99 percent Muslim, and the government has closed nearly every registered Protestant church in the country.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Algeria, North Africa, Islam, Berber, Kabyle, Church Closures, WWL Top 25, Apostasy
PERSECUTION TIER: Very High
WWL RANKING: #20 (Open Doors 2026)

## Overview

Algeria is one of the sharpest reversals in Christian history. This land produced Tertullian, Cyprian, and Augustine, three of the most formative theologians the church has known. Algeria today is about 99 percent Muslim. Over the past decade the government has closed nearly every registered Protestant congregation in the country, most of them among the Kabyle Berbers. Open Doors ranked Algeria 20th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## The Ancient Christian Heartland

North African Christianity was an indigenous faith. Tertullian of Carthage, who lived from about 155 to 220, was among the first to write Christian theology in Latin and shaped the vocabulary of the Western church for centuries. Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, was martyred in 258. Augustine of Hippo, born in Thagaste in what is now northeastern Algeria in 354, became the most influential theologian in Western Christian history. His *Confessions* and *City of God* formed Catholic and Protestant thought alike for more than a thousand years.

This was also a martyr church. Perpetua and Felicity were killed in Carthage in 203 and are remembered across the whole church. By the fourth century North Africa had more Christian bishops than any other region of the Roman world.

## Islamic Conquest and Disappearance

The Arab conquest began in the seventh century. Egypt's Coptic church survived Islamic rule and persists today. North African Christianity did not. By the twelfth century organized Christianity had ceased to exist across the Maghreb. French colonization from 1830 brought Catholic missionaries back, mostly serving European settlers. When independence came in 1962, the settlers left and took most of the Catholic institutional presence with them.

## The Kabyle Revival

Something unexpected began in the 1990s. Among the Kabyle Berbers in the mountains east of Algiers, large numbers turned to Christianity. The Kabyle are culturally distinct from Arab Algerians, speak Tamazight, and have long guarded a separate identity. The turn toward the gospel drew on both genuine spiritual searching and a rejection of Arab-Islamic identity politics. Evangelical faith spread through house groups, personal networks, and radio.

By the 2000s the Kabyle Christian community was estimated in the tens of thousands. The Protestant Church of Algeria, known by its French initials EPA, registered congregations and operated with a visibility that would have been unthinkable a generation earlier.

## Government Response and Church Closures

The state hardened in the 2000s. Ordinance 06-03, passed in 2006, restricts non-Muslim worship and criminalizes any attempt to convert a Muslim. From 2017 the government ran a systematic closure campaign against EPA congregations, citing building-code and administrative violations. By 2024 the EPA reported that nearly all of its 47 registered churches had been sealed or shut, including its largest congregations in Tizi Ouzou and Oran. Church leaders have faced prosecution, fines, and suspended sentences.

Apostasy is not a crime in the penal code. Algerians who leave Islam still face pressure from family and community, difficulty registering marriages, and prosecution on unrelated pretexts.

## Current Status

Estimates of Algeria's Christian population run from 50,000 to 130,000, with most being Kabyle converts. The pressures are administrative rather than violent. Believers who lose a building keep meeting in homes, which is where much of the Kabyle church started. The legal space for open Christian community continues to narrow.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Very High (61/100), **Rank:** #16 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** 50,000 to 130,000, mostly Kabyle Berber converts
- **Primary Threats:** Church closures, Ordinance 06-03, prosecution of pastors, family pressure on converts
- **Legal Status:** Non-Muslim worship permitted only in registered buildings, and registration is effectively unobtainable
- **Churches:** Nearly all 47 registered EPA congregations closed or sealed since 2017
- **Notable History:** Home of Augustine, Tertullian, and Cyprian

COUNTRY: Angola
TITLE: Angola — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/angola
META DESCRIPTION: Angola is a majority-Christian nation with deep Catholic roots and a fast-growing Protestant community. Religious freedom holds, though unregistered groups face restrictions.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles

PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Angola is among the most Christian nations in Africa. Around 90 percent of the population identifies as Christian. The Catholic Church, planted by Portuguese missionaries in the late fifteenth century, remains the largest body, and Protestant denominations are growing quickly. Angola is not a persecution country. The government does exercise real control over religious organizations, unregistered groups have been shut down, and the country's history carries the weight of a church that was tangled up with colonial rule.

## Historical Roots

Diogo Cão reached the Kingdom of Kongo in 1483 and Catholic missionaries followed within a few years. King Nzinga a Nkuwu was baptized as João I in 1491, which made Kongo one of the first African states to adopt Christianity officially. The relationship that followed was genuine. Kongolese nobles sent sons to Portugal for education, and a Kongolese man, Henrique, was consecrated bishop in 1518, one of the first indigenous African bishops in church history. Kongo adopted the faith and made it its own.

Portuguese colonial rule deepened over later centuries and used the Catholic Church as an arm of administration. Independence came in 1975 after a long liberation war. The new MPLA government was Marxist and hostile at first, nationalizing church schools and hospitals and restricting religious activity. Angola never suppressed the church the way other Marxist states did.

## Post-War Recovery and Current Status

The civil war of 1975 to 2002 killed an estimated 500,000 people and displaced millions. Churches provided some of the only functioning social services in the worst-hit areas, which bound the population more closely to the faith. Christianity has grown steadily since the war ended.

The government restricts new and unregistered religious movements. In 2018 it moved against roughly 1,200 unrecognized groups and required re-registration under tighter criteria, with only a small number of bodies legally recognized. A ban on Jehovah's Witnesses was later lifted after legal challenge. These measures reflect state oversight of religion across the board rather than hostility to Christians specifically. Angola does not appear on the Open Doors World Watch List.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (12/100), **Rank:** #82 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 90 percent
- **Primary Denominations:** Catholic around 55 to 60 percent, Protestant and evangelical around 30 percent
- **Current Status:** Religious freedom with strict registration requirements
- **Historical Note:** The Kingdom of Kongo adopted Christianity in 1491

CATEGORY: Country Profiles

PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Argentina is one of the most Christian countries in the Western Hemisphere. Around 80 to 90 percent of the population identifies as Christian. The Roman Catholic Church has been dominant historically, and Pentecostal and evangelical churches have grown fast over the past forty years. The constitution guarantees religious freedom, and Christians face no systematic persecution.

## Christianity in Argentina

Spanish colonization in the sixteenth century established Catholicism. The Jesuit reductions in the northeast became models of organized Christian community among the Guaraní and left a lasting architectural legacy. Argentina gained independence in 1816. The Catholic Church held enormous social influence into the late twentieth century, while Protestant denominations also took root.

Evangelical and Pentecostal churches have grown sharply since the 1980s, particularly in poorer urban districts. Jorge Mario Bergoglio, born in Buenos Aires, was elected Pope Francis in 2013 and served until his death on 21 April 2025. He was the first pope from the Americas. His successor, Leo XIV, was elected on 8 May 2025.

## Current Status

The constitution gives the Catholic Church a formally recognized status, which functions as a statement of heritage rather than an establishment that limits others. All religious groups operate freely. There is no persecution, no meaningful restriction on worship or evangelism, and no pattern of violence against Christians.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (7/100), **Rank:** #117 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** roughly 80 to 90 percent, Catholic majority with a growing evangelical minority
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom, no persecution
- **Notable:** Pope Francis, the first pope from the Americas, was born in Buenos Aires and died in 2025

COUNTRY: Armenia
TITLE: Armenia — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/armenia
META DESCRIPTION: Armenia was the first nation to adopt Christianity as its state religion in 301 AD. Its faith has been forged through invasion, genocide, and Soviet atheism, and remains central to national identity.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Armenia, Caucasus, Armenian Apostolic, Christian Nation, Genocide, Soviet History, Nagorno-Karabakh
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Armenia was the first nation in the world to adopt Christianity as its official religion. King Tiridates III was baptized in 301, twelve years before the Edict of Milan. That decision set the terms of Armenian identity for seventeen centuries. Being Armenian and being Christian have been bound together ever since. Persian invasion, Arab conquest, Ottoman massacre, and Soviet atheism each tested that bond, and it held at enormous cost.

## The First Christian Nation

Tradition credits the apostles Thaddaeus and Bartholomew with bringing the faith to Armenia in the first century. Gregory the Illuminator, imprisoned thirteen years by Tiridates III before the king converted, founded the Armenian Apostolic Church and is venerated as its patron. That church has maintained an unbroken institutional life for seventeen hundred years.

The Armenian church developed apart from both Rome and Constantinople. It rejected the Council of Chalcedon in 451, which placed it in the Oriental Orthodox communion alongside the Coptic, Ethiopian, and Syriac churches. Mesrop Mashtots created the Armenian alphabet around 405 specifically to translate Scripture and liturgy. That alphabet gave Armenians a literary culture rooted in their faith and shielded them from absorption into Persian, Greek, and Arabic worlds.

## Centuries of Persecution

At the Battle of Avarayr in 451, Armenian forces fought the Sassanid Persians over the right to remain Christian. They lost the battle and won terms that preserved the faith. Vartan Mamikonian, who led them, is venerated as a saint.

Under Arab rule from the seventh to the ninth centuries, Armenian Christians paid the dhimmi tax and were generally allowed to practice. Seljuk invasions in the eleventh century destroyed many historic churches. Under the Ottomans, Armenians lived as a recognized millet with limited self-government, subject to periodic massacre and systematic discrimination.

The worst came in the twentieth century. The Armenian Genocide of 1915 to 1923 killed an estimated 600,000 to 1.5 million people. Christian identity was central to the targeting. The genocide scattered Armenians across the globe and left wounds that have not closed.

## Soviet Period and Independence

Soviet rule from 1920 to 1991 brought state atheism. Church property was confiscated, clergy were watched and sometimes imprisoned, and public practice was curtailed. The church survived because it was too deeply fused with national identity to remove. Armenia declared independence in 1991 and restored the Apostolic Church to its former public role.

## Current Status and Tensions

Armenia has legal religious freedom. The Armenian Apostolic Church is named in the constitution and holds privileged standing, which does not amount to persecution of other Christians. Minority groups, particularly Jehovah's Witnesses, faced real difficulties in the 1990s and early 2000s, and their position has improved since.

The Azerbaijani offensive of September 2023 displaced almost the entire Armenian population of Nagorno-Karabakh, more than 100,000 people, within a week. Open Doors reported that at least ten Armenian Apostolic church buildings in the region were closed or destroyed during the following year. The fate of the remaining churches, monasteries, and cemeteries under Azerbaijani control is a live concern.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (9/100), **Rank:** #96 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 97 percent, predominantly Armenian Apostolic
- **Current Status:** Religious freedom, privileged status for the Apostolic Church, minority denominations generally free
- **Historical Note:** First nation to adopt Christianity as state religion in 301. The 1915 genocide killed up to 1.5 million Armenian Christians

## Overview

Azerbaijan presents itself as a modern secular state. The government also controls religious life closely. Christian communities live with registration requirements, surveillance, raids, and deportation of foreign workers. The population is about 97 percent Muslim, predominantly Shia, reflecting long Persian influence. The Christian minority includes Russian Orthodox, Armenian Apostolic, and Protestant communities. Open Doors placed Azerbaijan at 52 on the 2026 World Watch List, just outside the top 50, with no change in score from the previous year.

## Historical Roots of Christianity

Christianity came to the Caucasus early. The kingdom of Caucasian Albania, unrelated to the Balkan nation, covered much of present-day Azerbaijan and adopted Christianity in the fourth century. Apostolic tradition credits Bartholomew and Thaddaeus with the first preaching there. Persian dominance and the Safavid conversion of the population to Shia Islam in the sixteenth century reduced Christianity to a small minority.

## Government Control of Religion

Since independence in 1991 the government has managed religion through the State Committee on Work with Religious Organizations. Every religious community must register. Unregistered activity is illegal and draws fines, closure, and arrest.

A 2011 law tightened matters further. It banned religious education of minors outside approved settings and required state approval for all religious literature before distribution. Protestant house churches have been raided. Foreign missionaries have been detained and deported. Open Doors reports that surveillance is heavy enough that believers struggle to know whom to trust.

## Nagorno-Karabakh

Nagorno-Karabakh was populated predominantly by ethnic Armenians and held dozens of ancient Armenian churches and monasteries. A brief Azerbaijani military operation in September 2023 caused the flight of more than 100,000 Armenian Christians to Armenia within days. In the year that followed, at least ten public Christian buildings in the region, mainly Armenian Apostolic, were closed and destroyed. The fate of the remaining Christian heritage sites under Azerbaijani control is uncertain.

## Current Status

Physical violence against Christians in Azerbaijan is rare. Pressure comes through law and administration, including registration, literature controls, surveillance, fines, and deportation. Converts from Islam face pressure from family, friends, and neighbors.

- **Persecution Score:** High (44/100), **Rank:** #33 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 3 percent
- **Primary Threats:** Registration requirements, raids, deportation of missionaries, surveillance, family pressure on converts
- **Current Status:** Heavily restricted, rarely violent
- **Critical Event:** More than 100,000 Armenian Christians displaced from Nagorno-Karabakh in September 2023, with church buildings destroyed since

COUNTRY: Bahrain
TITLE: Bahrain — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/bahrain
META DESCRIPTION: Bahrain is a small Gulf kingdom with a Muslim majority. Christians, mostly expatriate workers, worship in designated buildings, and evangelism to Muslims is forbidden.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Bahrain, Gulf States, Islam, Expat Christians, Religious Restrictions, Middle East
PERSECUTION TIER: Medium

## Overview

Bahrain is the smallest and, by regional standards, the most religiously open of the Gulf Arab states. The population is majority Muslim, with a Shia majority and a Sunni ruling family. A large expatriate workforce includes many Christians from South Asia, the Philippines, Europe, and North America. Christians worship in designated buildings and are generally safe from physical harm. Proselytism directed at Muslims is illegal, conversion carries heavy social cost, and non-Muslim religious life sits firmly under Islamic governance.

## Christianity in Bahrain

Bahrain has no significant indigenous Christian community. The church here is almost entirely foreign. Manama has established Catholic, Anglican, and Protestant congregations serving that population. The Catholic cathedral Our Lady of Arabia, consecrated in Awali in 2021, is the largest Catholic church in the Gulf and serves the vicariate covering Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia. These churches may hold services and provide pastoral care. They may not evangelize Bahraini Muslims.

## Current Status

The constitution makes Islam the state religion and sharia a principal source of legislation. Apostasy is not written into the penal code. Leaving Islam still brings family rejection, loss of employment, legal trouble over marriage and inheritance, and in some documented cases danger from relatives.

Regional conflict has reshaped daily life since hostilities between the United States and Iran began in February 2026. Bahrain hosts the U.S. Navy's Fifth Fleet, and the U.S. ordered departure of non-emergency government personnel in March 2026. Expatriate church attendance has been affected by departures and flight disruption.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Medium (31/100), **Rank:** #53 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 14 percent, almost entirely expatriate
- **Primary Restrictions:** Proselytism to Muslims illegal, heavy social cost for converts
- **Current Status:** Private and designated worship permitted, no systematic persecution
- **Notable:** Our Lady of Arabia in Awali is the largest Catholic church in the Gulf

## Overview

Bangladesh is the world's eighth most populous country and one of the most densely settled. Islam has been the state religion since a 1988 constitutional amendment, and about 91 percent of the population is Muslim. Christians make up roughly 0.4 percent, close to 700,000 people. They carry several pressures at once. Islamist violence targets pastors and churches. Converts from Islam face severe family and community persecution. Discrimination in employment and education is routine. Open Doors ranked Bangladesh 33rd on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Historical Roots of Christianity

Christianity reached Bengal mainly through the colonial period. Portuguese Augustinian missionaries were present by the sixteenth century. British Protestant missionaries arrived in the nineteenth, and the Baptist Missionary Society and Methodists established churches, schools, and hospitals. The faith took deepest root among the indigenous peoples of the Chittagong Hill Tracts, including the Chakma, Marma, and Tripura, many of whom converted in large numbers. The Bengali-speaking Muslim majority was largely untouched.

## Creation of Bangladesh

Bangladesh was created in 1971 through a violent liberation war against Pakistani rule. The Pakistani army's campaign killed somewhere between 300,000 and three million people and affected Christian communities along with everyone else. The new state was constitutionally secular. That changed after 1975 as successive military governments moved the country toward political Islam. The 1988 amendment establishing Islam as the state religion marked that shift.

## Islamist Extremism and Violence

The years 2015 and 2016 were the worst in recent memory. Jihadist cells carried out a wave of machete killings against secular bloggers, Hindu priests, Christian pastors, and foreigners. Groups affiliated with al-Qaeda in the Indian Subcontinent and with Islamic State claimed the attacks. In July 2016 gunmen took the Holey Artisan Bakery in Dhaka and killed twenty-two hostages after separating them by whether they could recite Quranic verses.

Pastors have been attacked in their homes and on the street. Churches have been vandalized and bombed. In the Chittagong Hill Tracts, indigenous Christians face violence from both Islamist groups and Bengali settlers who take their land with official tolerance, which layers ethnic conflict on top of religious pressure.

The government prosecuted several militant cells after the 2016 attack and the security crackdown reduced organized attacks for a time. The conditions that produce the violence remain.

## Social Persecution and Converts

A Muslim who becomes a Christian in Bangladesh loses family, inheritance rights, and standing. In rural areas converts are driven from their homes, have property seized, or face organized community pressure that turns violent. Pastors who baptize converts draw threats from the convert's relatives and sometimes from local officials.

Employment discrimination is widespread. Christians report being passed over for government posts and excluded from business networks. Christian children face pressure to take part in Islamic religious instruction at school.

## Chittagong Hill Tracts

The indigenous Christians of the Hill Tracts face a distinct crisis. Bengali settlement, encouraged over decades, has displaced indigenous communities and taken their land. The military maintains a heavy presence. Human rights organizations continue to document land seizures, arson, and violence against these communities.

## Current Status

Sheikh Hasina's government fell in August 2024 after mass protests, and an interim administration under Muhammad Yunus took charge and set a path back to elections. The transition brought a spike in attacks on religious minorities, mostly Hindus, along with a broader loosening of restraint on Islamist political movements. Open Doors moved Bangladesh up its list in the years since. The direction of travel for religious freedom under the new political order is still unsettled.

## Current Status

The situation is stable rather than improving. The constitutional framework that disadvantages Christians is settled, the monastic establishment has no reason to seek change, and the government faces little pressure to reform.

## Overview

Burkina Faso was once held up as a model of Muslim and Christian coexistence in West Africa. A jihadist insurgency that began in 2015, tied to the al-Qaeda affiliate Jama'at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin and to Islamic State in the Greater Sahara, has made it one of the most dangerous places on earth to follow Christ. Churches have been burned by the hundreds. Pastors have been killed in front of their congregations. Whole Christian villages have been massacred. Across much of the north and east the visible church has ceased to function. Open Doors ranked Burkina Faso 16th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Historical Background

Burkina Faso, called Upper Volta until 1984, is a landlocked Sahelian country of roughly 23 million people. French colonization from the late nineteenth century brought Catholic missionaries who built missions, schools, and hospitals. About 61 percent of the population is Muslim, 24 percent Christian, split roughly evenly between Catholic and Protestant, and the remainder follow traditional religion. These communities lived together with unusual harmony for most of the country's modern history.

## The Insurgency

Armed groups moved in from Mali around 2015 and 2016. JNIM became the dominant force in the north and west. ISGS operated in the east along the Niger border. Both sought to impose their version of Islamic governance and to remove anyone they defined as an enemy, Christians included.

The pattern of attack has been consistent. Fighters enter a village, separate Christians from Muslims, demand that Christians renounce their faith, and kill those who refuse. Churches are burned as deliberate acts of erasure rather than as collateral damage. Catechists and pastors are singled out because they hold communities together.

## Documented Attacks

Militants attacked a church in Dablo in May 2019 and killed the priest along with five worshipers. More than a hundred church buildings were attacked, damaged, or destroyed that year. In February 2020 fighters attacked Pansi village, demanded that residents renounce Christianity, and shot those who refused. On Easter Sunday 2023 around 44 people were killed at Yatenga, with Christians specifically targeted. In February 2024 gunmen attacked a Catholic congregation at Essakane during Sunday mass and killed at least fifteen people, and a mosque was attacked in a separate incident the same day.

## The Humanitarian Crisis

More than two million people are internally displaced. A large share are Christians who fled the north and east when jihadist groups took control. Roughly 40 percent of the national territory sits outside effective government control. Inside those areas Christians cannot gather, carry Bibles, or show any sign of their faith.

## Government Response

Two military coups took place in 2022, bringing Captain Ibrahim Traoré to power. The junta expelled French forces, pivoted to Russian security support, and has armed large numbers of civilian auxiliaries. Its ability to protect rural civilians remains limited, and security forces have themselves been implicated in massacres of civilians. The government has also cracked down on critical voices, including Catholic clergy who spoke about the violence.

## The Church Today

Churches in Ouagadougou and other cities remain open and active. Organized church life across the rural north has largely collapsed. Pastors and priests were killed or fled, and congregations scattered into displacement camps where new congregations have formed. Burkinabè diaspora networks carry much of the prayer and advocacy load.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** High (54/100), **Rank:** #20 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 24 percent, roughly 5.5 million
- **Primary Threat:** Jihadist armed groups, JNIM and ISGS, targeting churches, clergy, and Christian communities
- **Displacement:** More than two million internally displaced, a large share Christian
- **Territory:** Roughly 40 percent of the country outside effective government control
- **Churches Destroyed:** Hundreds since 2015

## Overview

Burundi is a small, densely populated East African country that is around 90 percent Christian, one of the highest proportions on the continent. The Catholic Church has deep roots and heavy institutional presence, and Protestant, evangelical, and Pentecostal churches have grown substantially. Most Burundians worship freely. Real religious freedom concerns remain. Jehovah's Witnesses have been banned since 2019, some evangelical groups struggle to register, and the political crisis that began in 2015 left churches that criticize the government under surveillance and pressure.

## The 2015 Crisis and Church Suppression

When President Pierre Nkurunziza sought a disputed third term in 2015, mass protests broke out. Security forces crushed them. An estimated 1,200 people were killed and around 400,000 fled the country. Several Catholic bishops publicly opposed the third term. The government accused the church of political interference. Pastors and church leaders who sheltered protesters or spoke publicly were threatened, and the Catholic Church withdrew from monitoring the 2015 elections.

## Jehovah's Witnesses and Minority Groups

In 2019 the government banned Jehovah's Witnesses, ordered their dissolution, and prohibited their meetings. The stated reason was failure to meet registration requirements. Independent observers pointed to the group's refusal to take part in political structures as the real cause. Members were arrested for meeting and their properties were seized. The ban has remained in force. It is a genuine violation of religious freedom whatever one makes of the theological distance between the Witnesses and mainstream Christianity.

## Current Status

The large majority of Burundian Christians, Catholic and mainstream Protestant, worship without significant interference. The concerns are specific and targeted rather than general. Burundi is not in the Open Doors World Watch List top 50.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (14/100), **Rank:** #78 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 90 percent, Catholic majority
- **Primary Concerns:** Jehovah's Witnesses ban since 2019, political pressure on outspoken churches since 2015
- **Current Status:** Majority worships freely, specific groups restricted

## Overview

Cambodia holds one of the most dramatic survival stories in the modern church. The Khmer Rouge regime of 1975 to 1979 dismantled every part of Cambodian civil and religious life. By 1979 organized Christianity in Cambodia had been destroyed. What followed was a slow rebuilding from close to zero. Cambodia's Christian community now numbers an estimated 250,000 to 400,000 in a nation of about 17 million.

## Colonial-Era Christianity

Catholic missionaries arrived during the French colonial period and served a church that was largely Vietnamese in membership. The Christian and Missionary Alliance began work among Khmer people in 1923 and made real progress in Scripture translation and leadership training. By the early 1970s Cambodia had an estimated 60,000 Christians and a functioning institutional church.

## The Khmer Rouge Genocide

The Khmer Rouge took Phnom Penh on 17 April 1975. Their revolution aimed at Year Zero, a new society purged of everything connected to the old order. The genocide killed an estimated 1.7 to 2.2 million Cambodians, around a quarter of the population. Christianity was treated as a foreign religion tied to French colonizers and Western imperialism. Churches were closed at once, then demolished, burned, or repurposed. Clergy and church leaders were executed. The Catholic cathedral in Phnom Penh was dismantled stone by stone. By 1979 the church built over decades had been eliminated.

## Rebuilding

The United Nations Transitional Authority oversaw a democratic transition in 1991 to 1993 and Cambodia opened to the outside world. Christian organizations entered with humanitarian assistance and supported the rebuilding of the church. From near zero in 1979 the Christian population has grown to an estimated 250,000 to 400,000, perhaps 2 to 3 percent of the country.

## Legal Status and Social Challenges

The constitution guarantees religious freedom. Buddhism is the state religion and is woven into Cambodian cultural identity, and other faiths are permitted. A 2007 directive banned door-to-door evangelism and public proselytizing outside church premises, and it is enforced unevenly. In rural communities where Buddhist identity and village life are tightly bound, conversion is read as a rejection of community and ancestors. Converts face family pressure, social exclusion, and occasional community sanction. Pastors report periodic harassment from local officials.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (12/100), **Rank:** #82 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** 250,000 to 400,000, roughly 2 to 3 percent
- **Primary Faith Context:** Theravada Buddhist majority, about 96 percent
- **Primary Challenges:** Social pressure on converts, local harassment, restrictions on public evangelism
- **Legal Status:** Constitutionally free, with a standing ban on door-to-door evangelism
- **Historical Trauma:** The Khmer Rouge destroyed organized Cambodian Christianity between 1975 and 1979
