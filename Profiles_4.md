COUNTRY: Haiti
TITLE: Haiti — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/haiti
META DESCRIPTION: Haiti is overwhelmingly Christian and in a state of near collapse. Gangs control most of the capital, and clergy have been kidnapped for ransom and killed.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Haiti, Caribbean, Catholic, Protestant, Vodou, Gang Violence, Kidnapping, State Collapse
PERSECUTION TIER: Medium

## Overview

Haiti is around 85 percent Christian, roughly half Catholic and half Protestant, with Vodou practiced alongside Christianity by a substantial share of the population. There is no religious persecution in law. The threat is the collapse of the state. Armed gangs control most of Port-au-Prince and much of the surrounding region, and clergy are targeted for ransom because churches are assumed to hold cash and because congregations will pay.

## Church and Nation

Catholicism came with French colonial rule and was the official religion under the 1860 concordat. Protestant missions expanded through the twentieth century and now account for roughly half of Haitian Christians. Vodou was formally recognized as a religion in 2003. Many Haitians hold Christian and Vodou practice together, which Protestant churches oppose more sharply than the Catholic Church does.

The 2010 earthquake killed well over 100,000 people, destroyed the cathedral in Port-au-Prince, and killed the archbishop. Church networks handled much of the relief and still supply schooling and health care the state does not.

## Gangs and Clergy

Kidnapping for ransom became systematic after 2018 and worsened sharply following the assassination of President Jovenel Moïse in 2021. Priests, pastors, nuns, and seminarians have been abducted in groups. Ten people including seven clergy were taken in one 2021 incident. Six nuns were seized from a bus in January 2024. Church services have been attacked and worshippers shot. A pastor was killed during a service that was being livestreamed in 2024.

Some gang leaders use religious language and a few churches have been accused of accommodation with armed groups to keep operating. Schools and clinics run by churches close for long stretches when routes become impassable.

A Kenyan-led multinational security mission deployed from 2024 without restoring control of the capital. Elections have not been held since 2016.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Medium (20/100), **Rank:** #69 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 85 percent
- **Primary Threats:** Gang control of territory, kidnapping of clergy for ransom, attacks on services, state collapse
- **Legal Status:** Religious freedom protected. Enforcement of any law is minimal
- **Current Status:** State advisory level 4, do not travel. National state of emergency in force since March 2024

COUNTRY: Honduras
TITLE: Honduras — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/honduras
META DESCRIPTION: Honduras is overwhelmingly Christian with full religious freedom. Gang extortion and violence reach churches, and clergy who defend land rights face threats.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Honduras, Central America, Catholic, Evangelical, Gang Violence, Land Rights, Religious Freedom
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Honduras is around 85 percent Christian, split between a Catholic population and a large evangelical share approaching 40 percent. Religious freedom is constitutionally protected and there is no persecution.

Criminal violence is the practical problem. MS-13 and Barrio 18 extort businesses and churches in urban neighborhoods, and pastors have been killed for refusing payment or for pulling young people out of gang recruitment. Church youth programs in San Pedro Sula and Tegucigalpa operate under real risk.

A second pattern involves clergy who support campesino and indigenous communities in land disputes with agribusiness, mining, and hydroelectric interests. Priests and church workers in the Aguán valley and in Lenca territory have been threatened, and the 2016 murder of the activist Berta Cáceres, who worked closely with church networks, showed what those disputes can cost. Catholic radio stations covering land conflict have been attacked.

Evangelical churches hold considerable political influence, and church leaders have been prominent on both sides of the country's political crises since the 2009 coup.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (8/100), **Rank:** #102 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 85 percent
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom, no persecution
- **Live Threats:** Gang extortion of churches, threats against clergy in land rights disputes

COUNTRY: Hungary
TITLE: Hungary — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/hungary
META DESCRIPTION: Hungary has full religious freedom and a government that funds persecuted Christians abroad. Its church registration law has drawn criticism for favoring established denominations.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Hungary, Central Europe, Catholic, Reformed, Religious Freedom, Hungary Helps, Church Registration
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Hungary is around 43 percent Christian by the 2022 census, Catholic majority with a significant Reformed minority, and roughly 40 percent of respondents declined to answer. Religious freedom is protected and there is no persecution.

Hungary is unusual in state policy. The government created a dedicated office for persecuted Christians in 2016 and runs the Hungary Helps programme, which funds reconstruction and support for Christian communities in Iraq, Syria, Nigeria, Lebanon, and elsewhere. It is the only government with a ministry-level department for the subject, and the programme is cited approvingly by persecuted-church organizations and criticized by others as selective, since it directs aid on religious criteria.

The domestic criticism concerns the 2011 church law, which deregistered hundreds of religious organizations and required parliamentary approval for recognition. The European Court of Human Rights ruled against Hungary in 2014, and amendments followed that created tiers of recognition. Smaller Protestant, charismatic, and minority groups have less favorable access to state funding and tax benefits than the four historic churches.

Communist suppression from 1948 shaped the older generation. Cardinal József Mindszenty was tortured, tried in a 1949 show trial, and spent fifteen years sheltering in the U.S. embassy in Budapest.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (6/100), **Rank:** #131 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 43 percent by the 2022 census
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom
- **Live Issue:** Tiered church registration favoring historic denominations
- **Notable:** Hungary Helps funds persecuted Christian communities abroad

COUNTRY: Iceland
TITLE: Iceland — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/iceland
META DESCRIPTION: Iceland has an established Lutheran church, complete religious freedom, and among the lowest rates of active Christian practice in the world.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Iceland, Nordic, Lutheran, State Church, Secularization, Religious Freedom
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Iceland has an established church. The Evangelical Lutheran Church of Iceland is supported by the state and counts roughly 58 percent of the population as members, down from over 90 percent in 1990. Belief and attendance run far below membership. Religious freedom is complete and there is no persecution.

Iceland adopted Christianity by a decision of the Althing around the year 1000, when the assembly accepted the faith as a political settlement to avoid civil war while allowing private pagan practice for a period. Ásatrú, a modern reconstruction of Norse religion, is a registered faith with a growing membership and built a temple near Reykjavík.

A parish-tax system distributes funds to registered religious and secular life-stance organizations. Debate concerns whether an established church is appropriate rather than whether anyone may worship.

- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 62 percent by membership, much lower by practice
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom, established Lutheran church
- **Historical Note:** Christianity adopted by decision of the Althing around 1000

COUNTRY: India
TITLE: India — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/india
META DESCRIPTION: India holds tens of millions of Christians and one of the fastest-worsening persecution trends in the world, driven by Hindu nationalist mobs and anti-conversion laws.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: India, South Asia, Hindutva, Anti-Conversion Laws, Mob Violence, Dalit Christians, Manipur, WWL Top 25
PERSECUTION TIER: Very High

## Overview

India has between 28 and 65 million Christians depending on whose count is used, a small share of a vast population. The official census figure of roughly 2.3 percent almost certainly undercounts, because Dalits who convert lose affirmative action benefits and many do not register their faith. Persecution here is driven by Hindu nationalist movements rather than by a single state policy. Mobs attack prayer meetings, anti-conversion laws are used to jail pastors, and police often side with the attackers. Open Doors ranked India 12th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Ancient Roots

Tradition holds that the apostle Thomas landed in Kerala in 52 AD and was martyred near Chennai. The Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala are among the oldest continuous Christian communities anywhere and long predate European contact. Portuguese Catholicism arrived in the sixteenth century, and Protestant missions followed from the eighteenth, building schools, colleges, and hospitals that still rank among India's best.

Mass movements among Dalit and tribal communities from the nineteenth century produced most of India's Christian population. That origin shapes the hostility. Conversion is read by Hindu nationalists as the theft of low-caste communities from the Hindu fold, and as a foreign project.

## Hindutva and Anti-Conversion Laws

Hindutva holds that Indian national identity is Hindu, which places Christians and Muslims outside it. Organizations in the Sangh Parivar run *ghar wapsi*, homecoming ceremonies that pressure Christians and Muslims to return to Hinduism.

Twelve or more states have anti-conversion laws, variously requiring advance notice to district officials, criminalizing conversion by inducement or allurement, and placing the burden of proof on the accused. Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh have among the harshest versions, with long prison terms. In practice the laws are used to arrest pastors after a mob complaint, whether or not any conversion occurred.

Attacks run in the hundreds each year. The pattern is a prayer meeting in a home, a mob arrival, beatings, destruction of Bibles and furniture, and the arrest of the pastor rather than the attackers. The United Christian Forum has recorded over 700 incidents in single years.

## Manipur

Violence between the mainly Hindu Meitei and the mainly Christian Kuki-Zo communities erupted in Manipur in May 2023. Over 200 people were killed and tens of thousands displaced. Hundreds of churches were burned, including Meitei Christian churches destroyed by Meitei mobs, showing that religious identity was targeted alongside ethnicity. Central rule was imposed in February 2025, and displaced people remain in camps.

## Odisha and Earlier Violence

The Kandhamal violence in Odisha in 2008 killed around 100 Christians, destroyed thousands of homes and hundreds of churches, and displaced over 50,000 people. Convictions were few. The Australian missionary Graham Staines and his two young sons were burned alive in their vehicle in Odisha in 1999.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Very High (65/100), **Rank:** #15 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** 28 to 65 million, officially about 2.3 percent
- **Primary Threats:** Hindu nationalist mob violence, anti-conversion laws in 12 or more states, police complicity, loss of Dalit benefits on conversion
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #12
- **Major Events:** Kandhamal 2008, Manipur from 2023
- **Historical Note:** Saint Thomas Christians in Kerala date to the first centuries

COUNTRY: Indonesia
TITLE: Indonesia — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/indonesia
META DESCRIPTION: Indonesia has the largest Muslim population on earth and around 30 million Christians. Church closures, permit obstruction, and Islamist pressure define the difficulty.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Indonesia, Southeast Asia, Islam, Church Closures, Aceh, Papua, Blasphemy Law, Pancasila
PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

Indonesia holds the largest Muslim population in the world and roughly 30 million Christians, close to 11 percent of the country, concentrated in Papua, North Sulawesi, North Sumatra, East Nusa Tenggara, and among ethnic Chinese Indonesians. The state ideology, Pancasila, requires belief in one God and recognizes six religions including Protestantism and Catholicism, which gives Christianity formal standing. Pressure comes from local permit obstruction, Islamist mobilization, and blasphemy prosecutions. Indonesia fell outside the Open Doors top 50 for 2026.

## Church Building and Closure

A 2006 joint ministerial decree requires a congregation to gather 90 member signatures and 60 signatures from local residents of other faiths, plus approval from a local interfaith forum, before a church may be built. Hardline groups organize residents to withhold consent. Hundreds of churches have been closed, sealed, or blocked from construction since, and congregations that meet in homes or shops after being refused a permit are then closed for meeting without one. The GKI Yasmin congregation in Bogor fought for well over a decade, winning a Supreme Court ruling that local authorities simply ignored, before accepting a relocated site.

## Violence and Extremism

Suicide bombers attacked three churches in Surabaya during Sunday services in May 2018, killing over a dozen people, carried out by a single family including children. A bombing at the Catholic cathedral in Makassar on Palm Sunday 2021 injured around twenty. Sectarian conflict in Maluku and Poso between 1999 and 2002 killed thousands of Christians and Muslims and displaced hundreds of thousands. Security crackdowns have weakened Jemaah Islamiyah and affiliated networks, and the group formally announced its dissolution in 2024.

## Aceh and Papua

Aceh operates sharia by special autonomy. Its rules apply mainly to Muslims and include caning for various offenses. Christians in Aceh face the hardest church-permit environment in the country, and a wave of church closures in Singkil in 2015 followed mob action that killed one person.

Papua is majority Christian and the conflict there is about independence and resource control rather than religion. Papuan church leaders who document military abuses have been threatened and monitored.

## Blasphemy Law

Article 156a criminalizes insulting religion and has been used against Christians, including a Jakarta governor, Basuki Tjahaja Purnama, imprisoned for two years in 2017 after a doctored video of a campaign speech triggered mass protests. A revised criminal code passed in 2022 retains and broadens blasphemy provisions.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** High (41/100), **Rank:** #39 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 30 million, roughly 11 percent
- **Primary Threats:** Church permit obstruction and closure, Islamist mobs, blasphemy prosecutions, extremist bombings
- **Legal Status:** Protestantism and Catholicism are officially recognized under Pancasila
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** Outside the top 50

COUNTRY: Iran
TITLE: Iran — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/iran
META DESCRIPTION: Iran recognizes Armenian and Assyrian churches while treating Persian-speaking converts as a national security threat. House church leaders serve sentences of ten years or more.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Iran, Middle East, Islamic Republic, House Churches, Converts, Apostasy, Prison Sentences, WWL Top 10
PERSECUTION TIER: Very High

## Overview

Iran runs a two-track system. Armenian and Assyrian Christians are recognized minorities with constitutional protection, reserved parliamentary seats, and the right to worship in their own languages. Persian-speaking Muslims who become Christians have no legal existence as Christians at all, and the state treats their house churches as a security threat rather than a religious one. Charges are brought as actions against national security, with sentences of ten years and more. Open Doors ranked Iran 10th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Ancient Presence

Persia held some of the oldest churches outside the Roman world. The Church of the East was centered in Sassanid Persia and sent missionaries as far as China and India. Persian Christians endured waves of persecution under Shapur II in the fourth century and survived under Islamic rule as a shrinking minority. Armenian communities were resettled in Isfahan by Shah Abbas in the seventeenth century, founding the New Julfa quarter and its cathedral.

## After 1979

The Islamic Republic recognized Armenians, Assyrians, Jews, and Zoroastrians as protected minorities. Those communities may worship in their own buildings and languages. They may not evangelize, may not conduct services in Persian, and may not admit converts.

Persian-language services were banned. Bible printing and import were stopped. The Assemblies of God church in Tehran, which had held Persian services, was shut in 2013. Pastors have been killed. Haik Hovsepian Mehr, Mehdi Dibaj, and Tateos Michaelian were all killed in 1994 within months of each other.

House churches grew anyway. Estimates of Iranian believers of Muslim background range from several hundred thousand into the millions, and no figure can be verified. Satellite television and encrypted messaging carry teaching that cannot be delivered in a building.

## Arrests and Sentences

Raids follow a pattern. Security agents enter homes, seize Bibles, phones, and laptops, and detain members. Prosecutions run under Articles 498 and 499 of the penal code for membership of groups acting against national security. Sentences of five to ten years are common. Amendments to those articles in 2021 broadened the language further.

Pastor Yousef Nadarkhani was sentenced to death for apostasy in 2010, released after international pressure, and later imprisoned again on security charges. Many released prisoners face exile from their home provinces, travel bans, and forced attendance at Islamic re-education.

Converts cannot register a Christian marriage, cannot list their faith on identity documents, and lose custody and inheritance rights in disputes.

## Current Conditions

Hostilities between the United States and Iran that began in February 2026 have tightened internal security across the board. Periods of heightened security in Iran have historically produced more arrests of house church members, since the charges are framed in national security terms to begin with.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Very High (70/100), **Rank:** #7 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** roughly 300,000 to 1 million, mostly unregistered converts
- **Primary Threats:** Security prosecutions of house churches, long prison sentences, ban on Persian-language worship, apostasy exposure
- **Legal Status:** Armenians and Assyrians recognized. Converts from Islam have no legal standing
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #10

COUNTRY: Iraq
TITLE: Iraq — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/iraq
META DESCRIPTION: Iraq's Christian population has fallen from around 1.5 million to roughly 150,000 in two decades. Islamic State emptied the Nineveh Plain, and militia control has slowed the return.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Iraq, Middle East, Chaldean, Assyrian, Islamic State, Nineveh Plain, Exodus, WWL Top 25
PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

Iraq holds one of the oldest Christian communities on earth and is losing it. Estimates put the Christian population at around 1.5 million before 2003 and near 150,000 today, a collapse of roughly 90 percent in two decades. The 2003 invasion, sectarian civil war, the Islamic State occupation of 2014, and the militia control that followed each removed another layer. Open Doors ranked Iraq 18th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Ancient Church

Mesopotamia held Christian communities from the first centuries. The Church of the East, centered at Seleucia-Ctesiphon, ran schools and sent missionaries across Asia. Aramaic dialects descended from the language Jesus spoke are still used liturgically and spoken in some villages. The Chaldean Catholic Church, Syriac Orthodox, Syriac Catholic, and Assyrian Church of the East all trace continuous presence here.

## Collapse Since 2003

The fall of Saddam Hussein removed a repressive state that had nonetheless protected minorities from sectarian violence. Churches were bombed from 2004 onward. The Our Lady of Salvation siege in Baghdad in October 2010 killed over 50 people including two priests. Kidnapping for ransom targeted Christian families and professionals, who were assumed to be wealthy and known to be without militia protection. Hundreds of thousands left for Syria, Jordan, Lebanon, and the West.

## Islamic State and the Nineveh Plain

Islamic State took Mosul in June 2014 and marked Christian homes with the Arabic letter nun for Nasrani. Christians were given the choice to convert, pay the jizya, leave, or be killed. Around 120,000 fled the Nineveh Plain in days, most to Erbil and Dohuk in the Kurdistan Region. Churches and monasteries were destroyed, manuscripts burned, and the tomb traditionally identified as Jonah's was blown up.

Territory was retaken between 2016 and 2017. Reconstruction in Qaraqosh, Bartella, Karamles, and Bashiqa has restored many homes and churches, and Pope Francis visited Iraq in March 2021, including a service in the ruins of Mosul. Perhaps half of the displaced Christians have returned. Many will not, and emigration continues.

## Current Pressure

Iran-aligned militias within the Popular Mobilization Forces control parts of the Nineveh Plain, including checkpoints and local administration, and Christians report land seizures, property fraud, and intimidation. Political disputes over the recognition of the Chaldean patriarch and over Christian quota seats in parliament have further weakened the community's position. Kurdistan remains the safest region for Iraqi Christians.

- **Persecution Score:** High (53/100), **Rank:** #22 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** roughly 150,000, down from about 1.5 million before 2003
- **Primary Threats:** Militia control of the Nineveh Plain, land seizure, discrimination, continued emigration
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #18
- **Historical Note:** Church of the East, Aramaic liturgy, continuous presence since the first centuries

COUNTRY: Ireland
TITLE: Ireland — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/ireland
META DESCRIPTION: Ireland remains majority Catholic on paper while practice has collapsed. Religious freedom is complete and the church's authority has been undone by abuse revelations.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Ireland, Western Europe, Catholic, Secularization, Abuse Scandals, Religious Freedom
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Ireland is around 69 percent Catholic by the 2022 census, down from 94 percent in 1961, with the fastest growth in the category reporting no religion. Weekly mass attendance has fallen into the teens nationally and lower in Dublin. Religious freedom is complete and there is no persecution.

The change is driven by institutional collapse rather than by state pressure. Investigations into clerical sexual abuse, the Magdalene laundries, mother and baby homes, and industrial schools documented decades of abuse and concealment. State inquiries including the Ryan, Murphy, and Mother and Baby Homes commissions set out the findings, and the church and state have both issued apologies and paid redress.

Referendums removed the constitutional ban on divorce in 1995, legalized same-sex marriage in 2015, and repealed the Eighth Amendment on abortion in 2018, each passed against the position of the Catholic bishops. The church still runs the majority of primary schools, and the transfer of school patronage to multi-denominational bodies is a live policy question.

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (6/100), **Rank:** #131 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 75 percent nominal, with far lower practice
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom, no persecution
- **Live Issue:** Divestment of Catholic patronage of primary schools

COUNTRY: Israel and the Palestinian Territories
TITLE: Israel and the Palestinian Territories — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/israel-and-palestinian-territories
META DESCRIPTION: The land where the church began now holds a Christian population under 2 percent. Palestinian Christians face pressure from conflict and emigration, and Messianic Jews face restrictions in Israel.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Israel, Palestine, Holy Land, Palestinian Christians, Messianic Jews, Gaza, Emigration, Bethlehem
PERSECUTION TIER: Medium

## Overview

Christianity began here, and Christians are now under 2 percent of the population between the river and the sea. Around 185,000 Christians live in Israel, most of them Arab citizens, alongside roughly 47,000 in the West Bank and a community in Gaza that has fallen to a few hundred. The pressures differ by community and none of them is simple. Palestinian Christians face the effects of occupation, conflict, and economic collapse, plus pressure from Muslim neighbors in some areas. Messianic Jews face restrictions from Israeli authorities and hostility from some ultra-Orthodox groups. Both communities are shrinking mainly through emigration.

## The Historic Churches

Jerusalem holds the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, governed under a status quo arrangement shared among Greek Orthodox, Armenian Apostolic, Roman Catholic, Coptic, Syriac, and Ethiopian churches. Bethlehem holds the Church of the Nativity. The Greek Orthodox Patriarchate, Latin Patriarchate, and Armenian Patriarchate are among the largest landowners in the Old City. These institutions are ancient, contested, and politically exposed.

## Palestinian Christians

Bethlehem was a Christian-majority town within living memory and is now predominantly Muslim, the result of emigration rather than expulsion. Christians leave for the reasons other Palestinians leave, compounded by the fact that many have relatives abroad and the means to go. Movement restrictions, permit requirements for travel to Jerusalem, the separation barrier, settlement expansion, and a collapsed tourism economy all bear on daily life. Christians in the West Bank also report land disputes and pressure from powerful Muslim clans, matters the Palestinian Authority handles inconsistently.

Gaza's Christian community, historically several thousand, has fallen to a few hundred. The Greek Orthodox Church of Saint Porphyrius and the Holy Family Catholic parish sheltered hundreds of civilians during the war that began in October 2023, and both were struck, with deaths at each. Hamas rule since 2007 brought its own pressure on the community, including a ban on some public Christian activity and the unsolved killing of the Baptist bookshop manager Rami Ayyad in 2007.

## Messianic Jews and Evangelicals in Israel

Israel guarantees freedom of worship and protects the holy sites of all faiths. Messianic Jewish congregations, numbering in the low hundreds, operate legally and face a different set of problems. The Law of Return has been interpreted by Israeli courts to exclude Jews who profess faith in Jesus, so Messianic Jews have been denied citizenship on that basis. A 1977 law prohibits offering material inducement to convert, and repeated bills to criminalize evangelism more broadly have been introduced and dropped. The group Yad L'Achim campaigns against Messianic congregations, and some congregations report vandalism and harassment.

Clergy in Jerusalem have reported an increase in spitting and verbal abuse by some ultra-Orthodox individuals, and church property has been vandalized, including arson at the Church of the Multiplication at Tabgha in 2015.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Medium (24/100), **Rank:** #62 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 185,000 in Israel, roughly 47,000 in the West Bank, a few hundred in Gaza
- **Primary Pressures:** Conflict and economic collapse, emigration, movement restrictions, clan pressure in parts of the West Bank, citizenship and evangelism restrictions on Messianic Jews
- **Current Status:** Israel at State advisory level 3, Gaza and the West Bank at level 4 and 3 respectively
- **Historical Note:** The Holy Sepulchre status quo, the Church of the Nativity, continuous Christian presence since the first century

COUNTRY: Italy
TITLE: Italy — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/italy
META DESCRIPTION: Italy is the historic center of Western Christianity with full religious freedom. Practice has declined sharply and church vandalism is recorded in the hundreds each year.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Italy, Southern Europe, Catholic, Vatican, Secularization, Church Vandalism, Religious Freedom
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Italy is around 75 percent Catholic by identification, with weekly mass attendance near 20 percent and falling, and a growing share reporting no religion. Religious freedom is complete. Italy surrounds Vatican City, the seat of the Roman Catholic Church and a sovereign state since the Lateran Treaty of 1929.

The Catholic Church holds a concordat relationship with the Italian state, including the otto per mille tax allocation and the teaching of Catholic religion in state schools on an opt-out basis. Minority faiths negotiate separate agreements, and some groups, notably Muslim communities, have never obtained one, which limits their access to the same funding and legal recognition.

Several hundred acts of vandalism and theft against churches are recorded each year, and Italian church bodies track them. Motives are mixed, and prosecutions are uncommon. A small number of jihadist plots against churches have been disrupted, and Pope Francis was a stated target in intelligence reporting on more than one occasion.

Pope Francis died on 21 April 2025 and Leo XIV was elected on 8 May 2025.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (7/100), **Rank:** #118 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 75 percent nominal, roughly 20 percent practicing
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom
- **Live Issues:** Church vandalism, unequal concordat access for minority faiths
- **Notable:** Vatican City, an independent state since 1929, sits within Rome

COUNTRY: Japan
TITLE: Japan — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/japan
META DESCRIPTION: Japan is about 1 percent Christian despite full religious freedom. The faith survived 250 years underground and now faces indifference and social pressure rather than repression.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Japan, East Asia, Hidden Christians, Kakure Kirishitan, Nagasaki, Secularization, Converts
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Japan is roughly 1 to 1.5 percent Christian, one of the lowest shares in the developed world, and has complete religious freedom. The obstacle is social and cultural. Family obligation, Buddhist funeral rites, Shinto community observance, and long working hours all press against church commitment. Converts often face quiet family opposition rather than hostility, particularly over responsibility for ancestral graves and memorial rites.

## The Hidden Christians

Francis Xavier arrived in 1549 and the Jesuit mission grew quickly, reaching perhaps 300,000 believers by the early 1600s, with Nagasaki as its center. The Tokugawa shogunate then banned Christianity. Twenty-six Christians were crucified at Nagasaki in 1597. The Shimabara rebellion of 1637 and 1638 ended with the massacre of tens of thousands. Suspects were made to trample on images of Christ, the fumi-e test, and those who refused were tortured and killed.

Japan closed to the outside world for over two centuries. Christianity was thought extinct. When French missionaries built a church in Nagasaki in 1865, a group of local people approached a priest and identified themselves as believers. The Kakure Kirishitan had kept a form of the faith across seven generations without clergy, Scripture, or contact with any church, passing down prayers in memorized Latin and Portuguese fragments. The hidden Christian sites of the Nagasaki region were inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage in 2018.

Persecution resumed briefly after 1865 before the Meiji government lifted the ban in 1873. The atomic bomb dropped on Nagasaki in 1945 detonated near Urakami Cathedral, the heart of Japanese Catholicism, killing a large share of the community that had survived everything else.

## Today

Christianity carries cultural influence out of proportion to its numbers, through schools, universities, hospitals, and the popularity of Christian-style weddings. Church membership is aging and many congregations are small and struggling to find pastors. The Unification Church controversy following the 2022 assassination of Shinzo Abe led to a government dissolution request against that organization and to broader public suspicion of religious groups, which mainstream churches have felt as a chill.

- **Primary Constraints:** Social and family pressure on converts, cultural indifference, aging congregations
- **Historical Note:** The Kakure Kirishitan kept the faith for over 250 years without clergy

## Overview

Jordan is around 2 percent Christian, roughly 150,000 to 200,000 people, most of them Arab Christians whose families have been here for centuries, predominantly Greek Orthodox with Catholic and Protestant minorities. The state protects these historic churches, reserves nine parliamentary seats for Christians, and the monarchy presents itself as a guardian of Christian holy sites. Converts from Islam get none of that protection. Open Doors ranked Jordan 49th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Recognized Churches

Jordan holds sites of real significance, including Bethany beyond the Jordan, identified as the place of Jesus's baptism, and Mount Nebo. Recognized denominations own property, run schools and hospitals, and hold their own courts for personal status matters. Christian holidays are public holidays. Interfaith relations at the social level are generally good, and Jordan has absorbed large numbers of Iraqi and Syrian Christian refugees.

Some Protestant and evangelical groups are registered as societies rather than churches, which limits their property rights and legal standing.

## Converts

Sharia courts have jurisdiction over personal status for Muslims, and a Muslim who converts is still treated as a Muslim in law. Conversion can void a marriage, remove custody of children, and cancel inheritance rights. Religion is recorded on identity documents and cannot be changed from Islam to Christianity.

Family response is the greater danger. Converts have been beaten, confined, disowned, and forced to leave the country. Churches are cautious about accepting them because baptizing a convert can bring legal trouble for the church and violence for the person. Most Jordanian believers from a Muslim background meet in small private groups rather than joining an established congregation.

## Current Conditions

Jordan sits between Israel, Syria, Iraq, and Saudi Arabia and has absorbed shocks from every direction. Non-emergency U.S. government personnel and their families were ordered to leave in March 2026 following the onset of hostilities between the United States and Iran, and the country moved to advisory level 3.

PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

Kazakhstan is around 25 percent Christian, mostly Russian Orthodox among ethnic Russians, Ukrainians, and Germans, with smaller Catholic, Baptist, Lutheran, and Pentecostal communities. Around 70 percent of the population is Muslim, mainly ethnic Kazakhs. The state is officially secular and controls religious activity tightly through registration. Open Doors ranked Kazakhstan 45th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## The Religion Law

The 2011 Law on Religious Activity and Religious Associations forced every religious organization to re-register under raised thresholds, which cut the number of registered groups by roughly a third. Unregistered religious activity is an administrative offense. Groups need 50 adult members for local registration, 500 across two regions for regional, and 5,000 for national status, which puts national registration out of reach for most Protestant denominations.

Enforcement is routine. Police raid house church meetings, fine leaders and participants, seize literature, and order groups to stop meeting. Religious literature may only be sold at approved outlets and must pass state expert review. Sharing faith outside registered premises can be charged as illegal missionary activity, and foreign missionaries require annual registration that is frequently refused.

## Converts and Social Pressure

Ethnic Kazakhs who become Christians face pressure framed in national terms, that to be Kazakh is to be Muslim. Converts report family rejection, divorce, and pressure from village elders, and in some regions their burial in community cemeteries has been refused. Ethnic Russians who attend Orthodox churches face none of this.

## Context

Kazakhstan hosts a triennial Congress of Leaders of World and Traditional Religions in Astana, attended by Pope Francis in 2022, and presents itself internationally as a model of religious harmony. The registration regime and the raids run alongside that presentation. The January 2022 unrest and the subsequent political reshuffle did not change religious policy.

## Overview

Kenya is around 85 percent Christian, with a Muslim minority near 11 percent concentrated on the coast and in the northeast. Religious freedom is constitutionally protected and Christians dominate public life. The threat is al-Shabaab, the Somali jihadist group, which crosses into the northeastern counties and has repeatedly separated Christians from Muslims before killing them.

## Al-Shabaab Attacks

Kenya sent troops into Somalia in 2011 and al-Shabaab has attacked in retaliation ever since. Gunmen stormed Garissa University College in April 2015, singled out Christian students, and killed 148 people, the deadliest attack in Kenya since the 1998 embassy bombing. The Westgate Mall siege in Nairobi in 2013 killed 67, with witnesses reporting that attackers questioned people on Islamic knowledge. Buses have been stopped in Mandera and Wajir and passengers who could not recite the shahada shot.

Churches in Garissa, Mandera, Wajir, and along the coast have been attacked with grenades and gunfire. Non-local teachers and health workers, most of them Christians from other parts of Kenya, have been targeted so consistently that staffing in the northeast has repeatedly collapsed. Attacks continued through 2024 and 2025 at a lower tempo than the 2013 to 2015 peak.

## Other Pressures

Converts from Islam on the coast and in the northeast face family rejection and community violence. Kadhi courts handle Muslim personal status matters under the constitution, a long-standing arrangement some Christian leaders contest.

The Shakahola forest case in 2023, in which more than 400 bodies were exhumed after a pastor allegedly instructed followers to starve themselves, prompted a government push to regulate churches. Established denominations have resisted proposals they see as state overreach while agreeing that unregulated fringe groups are a genuine danger.

PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

Laos is a one-party communist state and around 1.5 to 3 percent Christian, roughly 150,000 to 200,000 people, in a country that is predominantly Theravada Buddhist. Persecution runs on two levels. The central government controls religion through registration and surveillance. Village and district authorities act with more force, expelling Christian families, confiscating land, and detaining believers, often without reference to national law. Open Doors ranked Laos 28th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Legal Framework

Only three Christian bodies are recognized, the Lao Evangelical Church, the Seventh-day Adventist Church, and the Roman Catholic Church. Groups outside these have no legal path to operate. Decree 315, issued in 2016, governs religious practice and requires official approval for gatherings, construction, printing, travel by clergy, and contact with foreign religious organizations.

## Village-Level Persecution

The hardest pressure is local. In rural areas, particularly among the Hmong and Khmu, becoming a Christian is treated as a rejection of the village's spirits and ancestors and therefore of the village itself. Documented responses include expulsion of families from their homes, confiscation of land and livestock, denial of access to the village water supply and school, refusal of burial in community ground, and forced signing of renunciation documents. Pastors have been detained for weeks or months on charges of disturbing social order. Families have been forced to live in the forest or relocate to towns.

Catholic and Evangelical congregations in Vientiane and other cities operate with far less interference. The gap between urban and rural experience is wide.

## Hmong Christians

Hmong communities carry the added burden of the Vietnam War, when many Hmong fought alongside American forces. The government has long treated Hmong areas with suspicion, and Hmong Christians are pressured as both a religious and a political problem.

- **Legal Status:** Three Christian bodies recognized. All other groups unrecognized
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #28
