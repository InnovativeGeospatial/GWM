COUNTRY: Latvia
TITLE: Latvia — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/latvia
META DESCRIPTION: Latvia is split between Lutheran, Catholic, and Orthodox communities with full religious freedom. Soviet occupation left a deeply secularized population.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Latvia, Baltic, Lutheran, Catholic, Orthodox, Soviet Legacy, Religious Freedom
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Latvia is roughly split among Lutherans, Roman Catholics, and Orthodox, with a large share of the population reporting no religious affiliation. Lutheranism dominates historically in the centre and west, Catholicism in Latgale in the east, and Orthodoxy among the Russian-speaking minority. Religious freedom is protected and there is no persecution.

Soviet occupation from 1940 to 1991, broken by German occupation, closed churches, deported clergy to Siberia, and enforced atheism for fifty years. Practice never recovered. Latvia is among the more secular countries in Europe by attendance.

Parliament passed a law in 2022 requiring the Latvian Orthodox Church to be fully independent of the Moscow Patriarchate, a security measure following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The church accepted the change and continues to operate. Registration law gives eight traditional denominations a distinct legal status, and smaller groups have complained about the ten-year probationary process required before a new denomination can form a national association.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (6/100), **Rank:** #131 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** roughly 60 percent nominal, far lower by practice
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom, no persecution
- **Live Issue:** Legally mandated independence of the Orthodox Church from Moscow

COUNTRY: Lebanon
TITLE: Lebanon — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/lebanon
META DESCRIPTION: Lebanon has the largest Christian share of any Arab country and a political system built around it. Economic collapse, war, and emigration are hollowing the community out.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Lebanon, Middle East, Maronite, Confessionalism, Hezbollah, Emigration, Economic Collapse
PERSECUTION TIER: Medium

## Overview

Lebanon holds the largest Christian share of any Arab country, roughly a third of the population, with Maronite Catholics the largest group alongside Greek Orthodox, Melkite Greek Catholic, Armenian Apostolic, and Protestant communities. The political system guarantees Christians the presidency, half the parliamentary seats, and senior state posts. No persecution exists in law. The community is shrinking anyway, through emigration driven by economic collapse, war, and the sense that the country has no future.

## A Confessional State

The 1943 National Pact set the presidency for a Maronite, the premiership for a Sunni, and the speakership for a Shia. The 1989 Taif Agreement that ended the civil war moved powers from the presidency to the cabinet and set parliament at parity between Christians and Muslims. No census has been conducted since 1932, because counting would force a renegotiation everyone fears.

Maronite Christianity has been here since the fourth and fifth centuries, taking refuge in the mountains and maintaining communion with Rome. The Armenian community grew through survivors of the 1915 genocide.

## Civil War and After

The civil war from 1975 to 1990 killed around 150,000 people. Christian militias were perpetrators as well as victims, including the Sabra and Shatila massacre of Palestinian civilians in 1982 carried out by Lebanese Forces militiamen under Israeli watch. Christian emigration accelerated during the war and never stopped.

## Current Collapse

Lebanon's currency lost most of its value from 2019, bank deposits were frozen, and the middle class was wiped out. The Beirut port explosion in August 2020 killed over 200 people and destroyed swathes of the historically Christian eastern districts, with no accountability since. The presidency sat vacant for more than two years before parliament elected Joseph Aoun in January 2025.

Hezbollah's dominance of Lebanese security and its war with Israel in 2023 and 2024 displaced over a million people and devastated the south and the Beqaa. Christian towns in the south and border areas emptied. Church institutions, which run a large share of Lebanon's schools and hospitals, have struggled to pay staff.

Estimates of Christian emigration since 2019 run into the tens of thousands, weighted toward the young and educated. That is the threat to Lebanese Christianity, and it is demographic rather than legal.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Medium (22/100), **Rank:** #66 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** roughly one third, largest Christian share in the Arab world
- **Primary Threats:** Economic collapse, emigration, Hezbollah dominance, war damage and displacement
- **Legal Status:** Constitutionally guaranteed Christian political representation
- **Current Status:** State advisory level 4, do not travel

TITLE: Lesotho — Christianity & Persecution Profile

## Overview

Lesotho is around 90 percent Christian, with Roman Catholics the largest body alongside the Lesotho Evangelical Church and Anglicans. Religious freedom is constitutionally protected and there is no persecution.

Christianity arrived in the 1830s when King Moshoeshoe I invited French Protestant missionaries from the Paris Evangelical Missionary Society, a deliberate choice by a ruler consolidating a nation. Catholic missionaries followed. Church bodies still run a large majority of primary and secondary schools under state funding arrangements.

The country is entirely surrounded by South Africa, has high HIV prevalence, and depends heavily on remittances and water exports. Political instability has produced frequent changes of government and periodic military interference, and church leaders have mediated between factions. None of this restricts worship.

COUNTRY: Liberia
TITLE: Liberia — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/liberia
META DESCRIPTION: Liberia is around 85 percent Christian and founded by freed American slaves. Churches were both refuge and target during two civil wars, and religious freedom holds today.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Liberia, West Africa, Christianity, Civil War, Americo-Liberians, Religious Freedom, Reconciliation
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Liberia is around 85 percent Christian with a Muslim minority near 12 percent. It was founded in the 1820s as a settlement for freed American slaves, and the Americo-Liberian settlers brought Baptist, Methodist, and Episcopal Christianity, which became the religion of the governing elite for over a century. Religious freedom is protected and there is no persecution.

The civil wars from 1989 to 2003 killed around 250,000 people. Churches sheltered civilians and were attacked for it. The Lutheran church massacre in Monrovia in July 1990, in which government soldiers killed around 600 displaced people sheltering in Saint Peter's Lutheran Church, remains the single worst atrocity of the conflict. Church bodies also led mediation, and the interfaith women's movement that helped end the second war included both Christian and Muslim leaders, work for which Leymah Gbowee shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2011.

Proposals to declare Liberia a Christian state have surfaced periodically, including in the 2015 constitutional review, and were rejected in favor of maintaining secular status. Muslim leaders opposed them and many Christian leaders did too. The Ebola epidemic of 2014 and 2015 killed thousands and disrupted church life severely, and churches carried much of the public health messaging.

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (7/100), **Rank:** #118 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 85 percent
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom, secular state
- **Historical Note:** The 1990 Saint Peter's Lutheran Church massacre. Interfaith peace work that helped end the second civil war

COUNTRY: Libya
TITLE: Libya — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/libya
META DESCRIPTION: Libya has no legal space for Libyan Christians. Militias abduct and kill migrant believers, and Islamic State beheaded twenty-one Coptic workers on a beach here in 2015.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Libya, North Africa, Militias, Islamic State, Migrant Christians, Coptic Martyrs, WWL Top 10
PERSECUTION TIER: Very High

## Overview

Libya has no functioning national government, two rival administrations, and dozens of armed groups holding territory. For Christians this means there is no law to appeal to. The tiny number of Libyan citizens who follow Christ practice in complete secrecy, and discovery brings death from family or militia. The larger Christian population is migrant, hundreds of thousands of sub-Saharan Africans and some Egyptian Copts, who are abducted, detained, extorted, and killed. Open Doors ranked Libya 9th on the 2026 World Watch List, down from 4th, on fewer verifiable incidents rather than on improvement.

## Ancient Christianity

Cyrenaica was a Christian region in the Roman period. Simon of Cyrene, who carried the cross, came from here. Synesius of Cyrene was a fifth-century bishop and philosopher. The Arab conquest of the seventh century ended organized Christianity across the region within a few centuries.

## After 2011

Gaddafi's fall in 2011 removed a repressive state that had at least maintained order. Islamic State established a presence in Sirte and Derna. In February 2015 the group released video of the beheading of twenty-one Coptic Christian construction workers, twenty Egyptians and one Ghanaian, on a beach near Sirte. The Coptic Orthodox Church canonized them as martyrs. Thirty Ethiopian Christians were killed in a further video that April. The bodies of the twenty-one were recovered in 2018 and returned to Egypt.

Islamic State lost Sirte in 2016. Militias, trafficking networks, and detention centers continue to prey on migrants.

## Migrant Christians

Sub-Saharan African migrants moving toward Europe are held in official and unofficial detention centers where UN investigators have documented torture, forced labor, sexual violence, and extortion. Christians among them report being singled out for worse treatment, pressured to convert, and punished for possessing Bibles or praying. Traffickers use religious identity as a lever for larger ransom demands.

## Libyan Believers

Conversion from Islam is not tolerated by family, community, or any armed group. Libyan believers number in the tens or low hundreds by any estimate and are entirely secret. A handful of foreign-serving churches operate in Tripoli and Benghazi for expatriates under heavy restriction.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Very High (69/100), **Rank:** #8 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** a very small number of citizens, plus migrant and expatriate communities
- **Primary Threats:** Militia violence, abduction and extortion of migrants, no rule of law, death for Libyan converts
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #9
- **Notable:** The twenty-one Coptic martyrs killed near Sirte in 2015

COUNTRY: Lithuania
TITLE: Lithuania — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/lithuania
META DESCRIPTION: Lithuania is the most Catholic of the Baltic states and kept its faith through Soviet occupation. Religious freedom is complete today.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Lithuania, Baltic, Catholic, Soviet Legacy, Hill of Crosses, Religious Freedom
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Lithuania is around 74 percent Roman Catholic, the most religious of the three Baltic states, and Catholicism is closely tied to national identity. Religious freedom is protected and there is no persecution.

Lithuania was the last pagan state in Europe, Christianized formally in 1387. Under Soviet occupation the church was suppressed, clergy deported, and the underground *Chronicle of the Catholic Church in Lithuania* published for two decades from 1972, smuggled to the West and read on Vatican Radio. The Hill of Crosses near Šiauliai was bulldozed by Soviet authorities at least three times and rebuilt each time by people who came at night to plant new crosses. It now holds well over 100,000.

The 1995 law on religious communities recognizes nine traditional religious communities with enhanced status, including Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Reformed, Jewish, Sunni Muslim, and Karaite. Newer groups apply for state recognition after a probationary period, and some, including a Baptist union and a neo-pagan association, have waited years or been refused, with the European Court of Human Rights ruling against Lithuania in one such case.

- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 80 percent, Catholic majority
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom, no persecution
- **Historical Note:** The Chronicle of the Catholic Church in Lithuania and the Hill of Crosses under Soviet rule

COUNTRY: Madagascar
TITLE: Madagascar — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/madagascar
META DESCRIPTION: Madagascar is around 85 percent Christian with religious freedom. Its church remembers Queen Ranavalona's persecution and now navigates chronic political instability.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Madagascar, Indian Ocean, Protestant, Catholic, Ranavalona, Political Instability, Religious Freedom
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Madagascar is around 85 percent Christian, divided between Catholic and Protestant bodies, with traditional ancestor veneration practiced alongside Christianity by much of the population. Religious freedom is protected and there is no persecution.

Christianity arrived with the London Missionary Society in 1818. Queen Ranavalona I banned it in 1835 and ran a persecution lasting decades in which believers were executed by spear, burned, or thrown from the cliff at Ampamarinana in Antananarivo. Estimates of those killed vary widely. The church grew through the period, and the martyr sites are marked by churches built after the ban was lifted in 1861.

The Malagasy church holds real political weight. The Council of Christian Churches has mediated in successive crises, and clergy have been prominent in disputes over election results. The country has endured repeated coups and constitutional crises, most recently the unrest of 2025, and church leaders were again involved in calls for a resolution. Instability, cyclones, and chronic poverty shape church work more than any restriction on worship.

COUNTRY: Malawi
TITLE: Malawi — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/malawi
META DESCRIPTION: Malawi is around 80 percent Christian with full religious freedom and strong interfaith relations. Churches carry a significant share of health and education services.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Malawi, Southern Africa, Presbyterian, Catholic, Religious Freedom, Church and State
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Malawi is around 80 percent Christian with a Muslim minority near 13 percent, concentrated along the lakeshore and in the south. Religious freedom is protected and interfaith relations are generally good. There is no persecution.

Scottish Presbyterian missions founded in the wake of David Livingstone's expeditions shaped the country, and the Church of Central Africa Presbyterian remains one of the largest bodies alongside the Catholic Church, Anglicans, and a growing Pentecostal sector.

The Catholic bishops' 1992 pastoral letter *Living Our Faith*, read from every pulpit, publicly condemned the one-party rule of Hastings Banda and triggered the movement that brought multiparty democracy in 1994. The bishops were threatened and the letter banned. Church bodies have remained politically consequential since, including in the successful legal challenge to the 2019 presidential election, which was annulled and rerun in 2020.

Churches and mosques both run schools and clinics. Attacks on people with albinism, driven by witchcraft beliefs, are a serious criminal problem that church bodies have campaigned against.

COUNTRY: Malaysia
TITLE: Malaysia — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/malaysia
META DESCRIPTION: Malaysia guarantees religious freedom while making it illegal for Malays to leave Islam. Churches face Bible confiscations, word restrictions, and the unsolved abduction of Pastor Raymond Koh.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Malaysia, Southeast Asia, Islam, Sharia, Bumiputera, Allah Word Ban, Raymond Koh, Sabah Sarawak
PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

Malaysia is around 9 percent Christian, roughly 3 million people, concentrated in Sabah and Sarawak on Borneo and among ethnic Chinese and Indian Malaysians. The constitution guarantees religious freedom and simultaneously defines a Malay as a person who professes Islam, which makes leaving Islam a legal impossibility for the majority ethnic group. Sharia courts handle Muslim personal status and apostasy cases. Open Doors placed Malaysia just outside the 2026 top 50.

## The Legal Structure

Islam is the religion of the federation. Every state except one has laws criminalizing propagating any religion other than Islam to Muslims, with penalties including fines and imprisonment. Malays who wish to convert must apply to a sharia court, which typically refuses, orders religious rehabilitation, or takes years without deciding. The Lina Joy case, decided by the Federal Court in 2007, confirmed that a Malay woman could not have Islam removed from her identity card by civil courts.

Sabah and Sarawak have distinct histories and larger Christian populations, and Christianity there is indigenous among Kadazan-Dusun, Iban, Bidayuh, and other communities rather than being an immigrant faith.

## Bibles and the Word Allah

Malay-language Bibles use *Allah* for God, as Malay-speaking Christians have for centuries. The government restricted the word to Muslim use, and thousands of Bibles were seized in 2014 by state Islamic authorities in Selangor. The Court of Appeal ruled against the Catholic newspaper *Herald* in 2013, and the High Court ruled in 2021 that a Christian woman could use the word in religious materials for personal use, a decision the government initially appealed. The position remains unsettled and varies by state.

## Disappearances

Pastor Raymond Koh was abducted from his car in Petaling Jaya in February 2017 in a coordinated operation captured on CCTV. The Human Rights Commission found in 2019 that Special Branch police were responsible and that the disappearance was state-enforced. He has never been found. The social activist Amri Che Mat disappeared in similar circumstances in 2016. The task force created afterward has produced no resolution, and Koh's family has continued litigation.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** High (43/100), **Rank:** #36 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 3 million, roughly 9 percent
- **Primary Threats:** Legal impossibility of Malay conversion, state anti-propagation laws, Bible and language restrictions, enforced disappearance
- **Legal Status:** Religious freedom guaranteed, with Islam constitutionally bound to Malay ethnicity
- **Notable Case:** Pastor Raymond Koh, abducted in 2017, still missing

COUNTRY: Maldives
TITLE: Maldives — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/maldives
META DESCRIPTION: The Maldives requires citizens to be Sunni Muslim by law. There is no legal space for Maldivian Christians, and the handful of believers practice in total secrecy.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Maldives, Indian Ocean, Islam, Citizenship Law, Secret Believers, Apostasy, WWL Top 25
PERSECUTION TIER: Very High

## Overview

The Maldives ties citizenship to religion. The constitution requires every citizen to be a Sunni Muslim, and a non-Muslim cannot be a citizen. There is no legal route for a Maldivian to be a Christian. The handful of Maldivian believers, a number no one can verify, practice in total secrecy and cannot gather. Open Doors ranked the Maldives 19th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## The Legal Position

The 2008 constitution and the 2015 Religious Unity regulations criminalize the practice or propagation of any religion other than Islam. Importing Bibles and religious items is prohibited. Public expression of any other faith is an offense. Conversion from Islam means loss of citizenship, and the penal code allows severe punishment for apostasy.

Foreign workers, who make up a large share of the labor force, may practice privately in their own accommodation and may not gather for worship or display religious symbols. Tourists in the resort islands, on which the economy depends, live under a separate and far looser regime that the government maintains by keeping resorts physically apart from inhabited islands.

## Society

Maldivian society is small and closely observed. Accusations of irreligion carry real danger, and the blogger Yameen Rasheed was murdered in 2017 after writing critically about religious extremism, following the disappearance of the journalist Ahmed Rilwan in 2014. Maldivians have travelled to fight for jihadist groups in Syria and Iraq at one of the highest per-capita rates in the world, and returnees remain a domestic concern.

Anyone discovered to have left Islam faces loss of employment, family rejection, detention, and physical danger. No church building exists and none is permitted.

- **Estimated Christian Population:** an unverifiable handful of citizens, plus foreign workers practicing privately
- **Primary Threats:** Citizenship tied to Islam, criminalization of non-Muslim practice, total social exposure
- **Legal Status:** Citizens must be Sunni Muslim. No legal Christian existence
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #19

COUNTRY: Mali
TITLE: Mali — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/mali
META DESCRIPTION: Mali's Christian minority has been driven out of the north and centre by jihadist groups. Churches have been burned, priests kidnapped, and Christian villages emptied.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Mali, Sahel, JNIM, ISGS, Jihadism, Church Closures, Wagner, WWL Top 25
PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

Mali is around 95 percent Muslim with a Christian minority near 2 to 3 percent, mostly in Bamako and the south. Jihadist groups have controlled or contested large parts of the north and centre since 2012, and the Christian presence there has been extinguished. Churches were burned, priests abducted, and believers fled. Open Doors ranked Mali 15th on the 2026 World Watch List and recorded it among the three countries with the highest violence scores.

## Collapse of the North

A Tuareg separatist rebellion in 2012 was rapidly taken over by Ansar Dine, AQIM, and MUJAO, which imposed their version of sharia across the north. Churches in Timbuktu, Gao, and Kidal were destroyed or converted. Sufi shrines and manuscripts in Timbuktu were also destroyed, and one perpetrator was later convicted at the International Criminal Court. French intervention in 2013 pushed the groups out of the cities without ending them.

JNIM, the al-Qaeda affiliate formed in 2017, and Islamic State in the Greater Sahara have since expanded into the centre and south. Village Christians have been ordered to convert, pay, or leave. Catechists and pastors have been killed. Sister Gloria Cecilia Narváez, a Colombian nun abducted in 2017, was held for nearly five years before release in 2021. Father Hans-Joachim Lohre was abducted in Bamako in 2022 and released a year later.

## Current Conditions

Two coups in 2020 and 2021 brought a military government that expelled French forces and the UN mission MINUSMA and brought in Russian Wagner and later Africa Corps personnel, who have been implicated in mass killings of civilians alongside Malian troops. Jihadist groups have tightened pressure on supply routes into Bamako, including fuel blockades, which shows how far the insurgency has advanced.

Christians in Bamako and the south still worship openly. Outside those areas the church has largely ceased to exist as a public body.

- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #15, among the highest violence scores recorded
- **Current Status:** State advisory level 4, do not travel

Malta is around 82 to 90 percent Roman Catholic, among the highest shares in Europe, and Catholicism is named as the religion of Malta in the constitution. Religious freedom is protected for all and there is no persecution.

Acts 28 records Paul's shipwreck on Malta and his stay on the island, which Maltese Christianity dates itself from. The Knights of St John governed from 1530 to 1798 and left the churches, fortifications, and Baroque architecture that define the islands.

Practice has fallen sharply in a generation. Sunday mass attendance has dropped from around 75 percent in the 1980s to roughly a third. Referendums and legislation have moved Malta away from church positions, with divorce legalized in 2011 and same-sex marriage in 2017. Catholic religious instruction in state schools is provided with an opt-out. Malta has one of the most restrictive abortion regimes in Europe, an area where church influence remains strong.

COUNTRY: Mauritania
TITLE: Mauritania — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/mauritania
META DESCRIPTION: Mauritania is an Islamic republic where apostasy carries the death penalty and no Mauritanian may legally be a Christian. The few believers are entirely hidden.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Mauritania, West Africa, Islamic Republic, Apostasy, Death Penalty, Secret Believers, WWL Top 25
PERSECUTION TIER: Very High

## Overview

Mauritania is officially the Islamic Republic of Mauritania and close to 100 percent Muslim. Citizenship is tied to Islam in practice, apostasy carries the death penalty, and there is no legal space for a Mauritanian Christian. The believers who exist, likely a few hundred at most, are entirely secret. Open Doors ranked Mauritania 21st on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Law and Practice

The penal code prescribes death for apostasy. A 2018 amendment removed the provision that had allowed a repentant apostate to escape execution, making the sentence mandatory on conviction. No execution for apostasy has been carried out in recent decades, and the law's existence does the work by itself. The blogger Mohamed Cheikh Ould Mkheitir was sentenced to death for apostasy in 2014 over an article criticizing the use of religion to justify caste discrimination, had the sentence reduced on appeal, and was held for years beyond his term before being released and leaving the country.

Proselytizing is criminal. Bibles in Arabic cannot be imported. A small number of Catholic and Protestant churches in Nouakchott and Nouadhibou serve foreign residents, mainly West African migrants and expatriate workers, on the explicit condition that no Mauritanian attends.

## Society

Mauritania abolished slavery in 1981, the last country in the world to do so, and criminalized it in 2007. Hereditary slavery persists in practice, particularly among the Haratine, and anti-slavery activists have been imprisoned. The social order that sustains it is defended in religious terms, which is part of why religious dissent is treated as dangerous.

A Mauritanian who is discovered to have converted faces family confinement, forced divorce, loss of all property and standing, and physical danger. Emigration is the usual outcome for those who are found out.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Very High (67/100), **Rank:** #12 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** a few hundred citizens at most, plus foreign residents
- **Primary Threats:** Mandatory death sentence for apostasy since 2018, criminal proselytism, total social exposure
- **Legal Status:** Islamic republic. No legal Christian existence for citizens
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #21

COUNTRY: Mexico
TITLE: Mexico — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/mexico
META DESCRIPTION: Mexico is overwhelmingly Christian and one of the most dangerous countries in the world to be a priest. Cartels kill clergy, and indigenous converts are expelled from their communities.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Mexico, Latin America, Cartels, Priests Killed, Chiapas, Indigenous Converts, Cristero War, WWL Top 50
PERSECUTION TIER: Medium

## Overview

Mexico is around 90 percent Christian and is one of the most dangerous places in the world to serve as a priest or pastor. Cartels kill clergy who preach against them, refuse extortion, or mediate between armed groups. A separate pattern in indigenous communities in Chiapas and Oaxaca sees Protestant converts expelled from their villages by community assemblies. Open Doors ranked Mexico 30th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Cartels and Clergy

Dozens of Catholic priests have been murdered in Mexico since 2006, and the Catholic Multimedia Center has tracked the killings for years. Two elderly Jesuit priests, Javier Campos and Joaquín Mora, were shot dead inside their church in Cerocahui, Chihuahua, in June 2022 while sheltering a man fleeing a gunman. Their deaths prompted the Mexican Catholic hierarchy to confront the government publicly over security policy.

Priests are targeted because they are immobile, known, and influential. Those who denounce recruitment of teenagers, run drug rehabilitation work, or attempt to broker local truces are the most exposed. Extortion of parishes is widespread in Guerrero, Michoacán, and Tamaulipas. Evangelical pastors face the same pressures with less institutional protection and less media attention.

## Indigenous Expulsions

In parts of Chiapas, community governance under *usos y costumbres* treats participation in traditional Catholic-syncretic festivals as a civic duty. Families who become evangelical and refuse to fund or join them have been fined, jailed, cut off from water and schooling, had homes destroyed, and expelled from their villages. Thousands have been displaced this way since the 1970s, with San Juan Chamula the best-documented case. State authorities frequently decline to intervene, citing indigenous autonomy.

## Historical Background

The Mexican Revolution produced a fiercely anticlerical constitution in 1917, and President Calles enforced it from 1926, closing churches and expelling clergy. The Cristero War that followed killed around 90,000 people. Priests were executed, including Miguel Pro, shot in 1927 and later beatified. Constitutional restrictions on the church were not substantially lifted until 1992.

- **Primary Threats:** Cartel killings and extortion of clergy, expulsion of indigenous converts in Chiapas and Oaxaca
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #30
- **Historical Note:** The Cristero War, 1926 to 1929

COUNTRY: Mongolia

## Overview

Mongolia has one of the youngest churches in the world. When communism ended in 1990 there were almost no known Mongolian Christians. There are now somewhere between 40,000 and 80,000, roughly 2 percent of the population, in a country that is predominantly Buddhist with a strong shamanic tradition and a large non-religious share. Religious freedom is protected and there is no systematic persecution.

Nestorian Christianity had a real presence among the Mongol tribes before and during the empire. Sorghaghtani Beki, mother of Kublai and Möngke Khan, was a Christian of the Church of the East, and Christian communities existed at the Mongol court. That presence disappeared centuries ago and has no continuity with the present church.

Communist rule from 1924 destroyed Buddhist monasteries, executed thousands of monks, and banned religion outright. The 1990 transition opened the country and foreign missionaries arrived quickly. Growth since has been rapid, mostly evangelical, with Mongolian-led congregations now the norm.

Friction is administrative and social. Religious organizations register with local authorities, and renewals are sometimes delayed or refused, with rules varying by district. Some registrations cap the number of clergy or restrict activity to a named building. Buddhist nationalist voices frame Christianity as foreign, and converts face family pressure over participation in Buddhist and shamanic family rites. Pope Francis visited in 2023, drawing attention to a Catholic community numbering only in the low thousands.

COUNTRY: Morocco
TITLE: Morocco — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/morocco
META DESCRIPTION: Morocco permits foreign churches while criminalizing any attempt to convert a Muslim. Moroccan believers meet in secret house churches and have no legal recognition.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles

PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

Morocco is over 99 percent Muslim, and the king holds the title Commander of the Faithful, which makes religious authority part of the constitutional order. Foreign Christians worship openly in established Catholic, Anglican, and Protestant churches. Moroccan citizens who become Christians have no legal recognition, meet in secret house churches, and face prosecution for any activity that can be construed as shaking a Muslim's faith. Open Doors ranked Morocco 23rd on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Article 220

The penal code punishes anyone who uses means of seduction to shake the faith of a Muslim or to convert them, with imprisonment and fines. The provision is used against Moroccan believers and against foreigners. Around 150 foreign Christian residents, many of them long-term aid and education workers, were expelled in 2010 in a coordinated operation, including staff of an orphanage in Aïn Leuh. Deportations have continued at a lower rate since.

Apostasy itself is not criminalized in the penal code. Leaving Islam still carries consequences through family law, since personal status is governed by Islamic rules, affecting marriage, child custody, and inheritance.

## Moroccan Believers

Estimates of Moroccan Christians range from a few thousand to around 50,000, with no reliable count. They meet in homes in groups of a few families. A small number have campaigned publicly for recognition, forming a coordinating body that has petitioned for the right to marry in church, give Christian names to children, and be buried in Christian cemeteries. None of those rights has been granted.

Converts report surveillance, questioning by police, employment discrimination, and family confinement. The pressure is heavier in rural areas and among Amazigh communities, where the group is small enough that word travels.

Morocco presents itself internationally as a model of religious tolerance, has restored synagogues and supports a Jewish heritage programme, and hosted a 2016 declaration on the rights of religious minorities in Muslim lands. The position of its own Christian citizens sits uneasily alongside that.

- **Primary Threats:** Article 220 prosecutions, deportation of foreign Christians, family and social pressure, no legal recognition
- **Legal Status:** Foreign worship permitted. Moroccan Christians unrecognized
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #23

## Overview

Mozambique is around 60 percent Christian with a Muslim minority near 19 percent concentrated in the north. The country appears on persecution lists because of one region. An insurgency in Cabo Delgado, launched in 2017 by a group known locally as al-Shabab and affiliated with Islamic State as its Mozambique Province, has beheaded civilians, burned churches and villages, and displaced close to a million people. Open Doors ranked Mozambique 39th on the 2026 World Watch List and among the worst countries in the world for abductions of Christians.

## The Insurgency

The first attacks hit Mocímboa da Praia in October 2017. The group grew rapidly in a province that is rich in rubies and offshore gas and poor in everything else. It pledged to Islamic State in 2019.

Attacks have targeted Christians explicitly in many cases, with villagers separated by faith, churches burned, and beheadings carried out in public. The assault on Palma in March 2021 killed dozens including foreign contractors and forced the suspension of a multibillion-dollar TotalEnergies gas project. Christian villages in Muidumbe, Macomia, and Mocímboa have been destroyed repeatedly. Women and girls have been abducted and forced into marriage.

Rwandan troops and a Southern African regional force deployed from 2021 and retook the main towns. The SAMIM force withdrew in 2024, Rwandan forces remain, and attacks have continued and spread south into Niassa and Nampula, including raids in 2024 and 2025 that displaced tens of thousands more.

## Elsewhere in the Country

Outside the north, Mozambique has religious freedom and no persecution. Catholic, Anglican, Presbyterian, Zionist, and Pentecostal churches operate freely. The Catholic Church mediated the peace process that ended the civil war in 1992 and the later agreements with Renamo.

COUNTRY: Myanmar (Burma)
TITLE: Myanmar — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/myanmar
META DESCRIPTION: Myanmar's military has bombed churches and displaced entire Christian populations in Chin, Kachin, and Karen states since the 2021 coup.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Myanmar, Burma, Military Junta, Chin, Kachin, Karen, Airstrikes, Displacement, WWL Top 25
PERSECUTION TIER: Very High

## Overview

Myanmar is around 8 percent Christian, roughly 4 million people, concentrated in the ethnic minority states of Chin, Kachin, and Karen, where Christians are a large majority of the local population. The military coup of February 2021 restarted a civil war that has hit those states hardest. Churches have been shelled and bombed from the air, clergy killed, and entire towns emptied. Open Doors ranked Myanmar 14th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Christianity and Ethnicity

Adoniram Judson, an American Baptist, arrived in 1813 and spent decades translating the Bible into Burmese with few converts among the Bamar Buddhist majority. The response came instead from the hill peoples. Karen, Chin, Kachin, and Naga communities converted in large numbers through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and Christianity became bound to ethnic minority identity.

That link is why the military treats Christianity as a political problem. The Burmese state has pursued *Burmanization*, promoting Bamar identity and Theravada Buddhism as the national norm since independence in 1948. Ethnic armed organizations in the Christian-majority states have fought the central government for most of that time.

## After the 2021 Coup

The military seized power in February 2021 and met nationwide resistance. Fighting spread into areas that had been calm for decades. In the Christian-majority states the army has used artillery and airstrikes against towns and villages.

Thantlang in Chin State was largely destroyed by repeated shelling and arson from 2021, with most of its churches burned and its population of thousands displaced. A pastor was shot dead there in September 2021 while trying to put out a fire. Airstrikes have hit churches sheltering displaced people, including a strike on a Catholic church compound in Kayah State in 2022 and a strike in Kachin State in 2023 that killed civilians at a displacement camp. Soldiers have occupied church buildings as bases, which then draws fire from the other side.

Cardinal Charles Bo, archbishop of Yangon, has repeatedly called on the military to stop attacking places of worship.

## Rohingya

The military's 2017 campaign against Rohingya Muslims in Rakhine State, which drove over 700,000 people into Bangladesh and is the subject of a genocide case at the International Court of Justice, is the clearest demonstration of what this army does to a religious minority it has decided is not Burmese.

- **Persecution Score:** Very High (66/100), **Rank:** #13 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 4 million, roughly 8 percent, concentrated in Chin, Kachin, and Karen states
- **Primary Threats:** Military airstrikes and shelling of Christian towns, destruction of churches, mass displacement, killing of clergy
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #14
- **Current Status:** State advisory level 4, do not travel
