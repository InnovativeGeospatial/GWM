COUNTRY: Cameroon
TITLE: Cameroon — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/cameroon
META DESCRIPTION: Cameroon is majority Christian but faces Boko Haram in the Far North and a separatist war in the Anglophone regions. Churches, pastors, and schools have been attacked from both directions.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Cameroon, Central Africa, Boko Haram, Anglophone Crisis, Church Attacks, Displacement
PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

Cameroon is roughly 60 percent Christian, and the threat here comes from armed conflict rather than from the law. Boko Haram and its ISWAP offshoot raid the Far North from across the Nigerian and Chadian borders. In the Northwest and Southwest, an Anglophone separatist war has run since 2017 and has killed thousands and closed schools for years at a time. Churches sit in the middle of both. Clergy have been kidnapped for ransom, congregations have been attacked during services, and church schools have been burned by separatists enforcing education boycotts.

## Historical Roots

German, then British and French colonial administration brought Catholic and Protestant missions from the late nineteenth century. Both built the school and hospital networks that still carry much of the country's education and health care. Christianity is concentrated in the south and west. The north is predominantly Muslim, with traditional religion throughout.

## Boko Haram in the Far North

Boko Haram began crossing from Nigeria in force around 2014. The Far North has absorbed hundreds of raids, suicide bombings in markets and mosques, and mass abductions. Christians in the region are a minority and are targeted specifically in some attacks, though the group kills Muslims who reject it in far larger numbers. Hundreds of thousands have been displaced inside the region.

## The Anglophone Crisis

Protest by Anglophone lawyers and teachers in 2016 over francophone dominance escalated into armed separatism by 2017. Separatist fighters seeking Ambazonia have enforced school boycotts by burning schools and abducting teachers and students, including at mission schools. Government forces have burned villages and carried out extrajudicial killings. Priests and pastors have been killed by both sides. Cardinal Christian Tumi, a leading church mediator, was briefly abducted in 2020 before his death in 2021. Church leaders who attempt mediation are treated as partisan by whichever side they criticize.

## Current Status

The October 2025 election and its aftermath added a further layer of unrest to a country already fighting on two fronts. Churches function normally across most of the south and the capital. In the Far North and the two Anglophone regions, congregations meet irregularly, clergy travel at risk, and displacement keeps breaking up church communities.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** High (41/100), **Rank:** #39 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 60 percent
- **Primary Threats:** Boko Haram and ISWAP in the Far North, separatist and army violence in the Anglophone regions, kidnapping of clergy for ransom
- **Displacement:** Roughly a million people displaced across the two conflicts
- **Legal Status:** Religious freedom protected in law

COUNTRY: Canada
TITLE: Canada — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/canada
META DESCRIPTION: Canada has full religious freedom and a large but declining Christian population. Debate centers on religious expression in public life rather than on persecution.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Canada, North America, Religious Freedom, Secularization, Catholic, Protestant
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Canada has constitutional religious freedom under the Charter of Rights and Freedoms and no persecution of Christians. Around 53 percent of Canadians identified as Christian at the 2021 census, down from 77 percent in 2001, one of the steepest declines recorded in a Western country. The share reporting no religion rose to roughly 35 percent.

## Current Issues

Public argument concerns the boundary between religious conviction and law. Quebec's Bill 21 bars some public employees from wearing religious symbols at work, which affects observant Sikhs, Muslims, and Christians. Conscience protections for medical practitioners have been contested as assisted dying expanded. Churches were among the institutions restricted during pandemic public health orders, and a small number of pastors were fined or briefly jailed for holding services in breach of those orders.

A wave of arson and vandalism struck Canadian churches in 2021 and 2022, with dozens of buildings burned or damaged, many of them Catholic and a number on Indigenous land, following reporting on unmarked graves at former residential schools. Those attacks were criminal acts rather than state policy, and prosecutions followed in some cases.

The residential school system is the heaviest item in Canadian church history. Churches, chiefly Catholic, Anglican, Presbyterian, and United, ran schools that removed Indigenous children from their families for over a century. Pope Francis travelled to Canada in 2022 and apologized for the church's role.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (7/100), **Rank:** #118 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 53 percent, falling
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom, no persecution
- **Live Issues:** Quebec Bill 21, conscience protections, church arson in 2021 and 2022
- **Historical Weight:** Church-run residential schools, papal apology in 2022

COUNTRY: Central African Republic
TITLE: Central African Republic — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/central-african-republic
META DESCRIPTION: The Central African Republic is majority Christian and has been torn by a decade of armed conflict in which religious identity became a marker for killing on both sides.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Central African Republic, Sub-Saharan Africa, Seleka, Anti-balaka, Armed Conflict, Displacement, Catholic
PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

The Central African Republic is around 80 percent Christian and among the poorest and least governed countries on earth. Conflict since 2013 has killed thousands and displaced a large share of the population. Religion became the dividing line when the mainly Muslim Seleka coalition seized power in 2013 and the anti-balaka militias formed against them. Christians were killed by Seleka fighters. Muslims were killed in large numbers by anti-balaka groups that claimed a Christian identity. Any account of this conflict has to carry both facts.

## The Conflict

Seleka took Bangui in March 2013 and deposed President Bozizé. Fighters attacked churches, killed clergy, and drove Christian communities out of parts of the north and east. Anti-balaka militias formed in response and carried out reprisal killings that amounted to ethnic cleansing of Muslim communities in the west. Church leaders, including Archbishop Dieudonné Nzapalainga and the imam Oumar Kobine Layama, worked publicly together to refuse the religious framing of the war. Nzapalainga was made a cardinal in 2016.

Armed groups still control large areas outside Bangui despite a 2019 peace agreement and the presence of the UN mission MINUSCA. Russian Wagner-linked forces have operated alongside government troops since 2018 and have been implicated in abuses against civilians.

## The Church Today

The Catholic Church is the most trusted national institution and runs much of the country's schooling and health care. Parishes have sheltered displaced families for years at a time, which has made them targets. Priests have been killed. In rural areas outside government control, church activity depends on whichever armed group holds the ground.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** High (40/100), **Rank:** #43 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 80 percent
- **Primary Threats:** Armed groups, collapse of state authority, reprisal violence
- **Displacement:** Hundreds of thousands displaced internally, with more as refugees in neighboring countries
- **Note:** Christians were victims and, through the anti-balaka, perpetrators

COUNTRY: Chad
TITLE: Chad — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/chad
META DESCRIPTION: Chad is split between a Muslim north and a Christian south. Boko Haram attacks the Lake Chad basin and converts from Islam face family pressure, though the state does not persecute Christians.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Chad, Sahel, Boko Haram, Lake Chad, Islam, Christianity, Converts
PERSECUTION TIER: Medium

## Overview

Chad is roughly 45 percent Christian and 52 percent Muslim, divided broadly between a Muslim north and centre and a Christian and animist south. The state does not persecute Christians and the constitution provides for religious freedom. Pressure comes from Boko Haram and ISWAP in the Lake Chad basin, from family and community sanction against converts from Islam, and from a security situation that has worsened steadily.

## Background

French colonial rule brought Catholic and Protestant missions to the south while leaving the Muslim north largely alone, a split that still shapes politics. Since independence the state has been dominated by northern elites. Idriss Déby ruled for thirty years until he was killed in 2021, and his son Mahamat Déby took power and later won a contested presidential election.

## Boko Haram and the Lake Chad Basin

Boko Haram and ISWAP operate across the islands and shoreline of Lake Chad. Attacks have killed soldiers and civilians in the hundreds. A March 2020 assault on a Chadian army position at Boma killed around a hundred soldiers. Attacks continued through 2025, and Chad has periodically threatened to withdraw from the regional force. Christians in the affected zone are a small minority and suffer alongside Muslim neighbors.

## Converts and Social Pressure

Chad is a secular state on paper, and the government has resisted Islamist political movements, at one point banning the face veil after suicide bombings in 2015. A Chadian who leaves Islam still faces the loss of family, inheritance, and employment. In the north this pressure is severe enough that conversion is usually kept hidden.

## Key Facts

- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 45 percent
- **Primary Threats:** Boko Haram and ISWAP in the Lake Chad basin, family and community pressure on converts
- **Legal Status:** Secular constitution with religious freedom protections
- **Current Status:** State advisory level raised to 4, do not travel, in 2026

COUNTRY: China
TITLE: China — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/china
META DESCRIPTION: China holds one of the largest Christian populations on earth under one of the most sophisticated systems of religious control. Sinicization, surveillance, and pastor imprisonment define the pressure.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: China, East Asia, Communist Party, Sinicization, House Churches, Surveillance, Three-Self, WWL Top 25
PERSECUTION TIER: Very High

## Overview

China may hold the largest Christian population in Asia and one of the largest anywhere, estimated between 60 and 100 million across registered and unregistered churches. The Communist Party does not try to erase Christianity. It tries to own it. Churches must register with the state-run Three-Self Patriotic Movement or the Catholic Patriotic Association. Those that refuse are unregistered and illegal, and their pastors are prosecuted, their buildings closed, and their members surveilled. Open Doors ranked China 17th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Historical Roots

Nestorian Christianity reached Tang China in the seventh century, recorded on the Xi'an stele of 781. Jesuit missionaries including Matteo Ricci arrived in the sixteenth century and worked at the imperial court. Protestant missions expanded through the nineteenth century under treaties imposed after the Opium Wars, which tied the faith to foreign coercion in the public mind, a link the Party still uses.

The Boxer Uprising of 1900 killed thousands of Chinese Christians and a smaller number of missionaries. After 1949 the new Communist state expelled foreign missionaries and created the Three-Self structure to sever Chinese churches from outside control. The Cultural Revolution from 1966 to 1976 closed every church in the country and sent clergy to labor camps. Christianity survived in homes, and the church that emerged after 1979 was larger than the one driven underground.

## Sinicization and Current Policy

Xi Jinping's administration launched sinicization of religion in 2015, requiring belief and practice to conform to Chinese culture and Party leadership. Revised Regulations on Religious Affairs took effect in 2018 and tightened control over venues, clergy, publishing, and online religious content. Minors are barred from religious instruction and attendance in most provinces.

Enforcement has been physical and digital. Crosses were removed from thousands of church buildings in Zhejiang. Facial recognition cameras were installed in registered churches. Sermons are expected to incorporate Party themes, and some churches display portraits of state leaders. Online religious content requires a license.

Early Rain Covenant Church in Chengdu was raided in December 2018 and its pastor, Wang Yi, was sentenced to nine years for inciting subversion. Zion Church in Beijing, once the largest unregistered congregation in the city, was shut in 2018. Arrests and fines of house church leaders have continued since.

The Vatican and Beijing signed a provisional agreement on bishop appointments in 2018, renewed several times since. Critics argue it gave the Party a formal role in choosing Catholic bishops and offered little to underground Catholics who had refused state registration for decades.

## Xinjiang and Tibet

The campaign against Uyghur Muslims in Xinjiang, involving mass internment, forced labor, and surveillance, shows what the state will do when it treats a religious community as a security threat. Tibetan Buddhists face a parallel system. Christians are not treated with that severity, and both cases are the reason Christian leaders take sinicization seriously.

## The Church Today

Registered churches function openly under supervision and are often full. Unregistered house churches meet in apartments and rented rooms, split deliberately into small groups to reduce exposure. Growth has slowed from the surge of the 1980s and 1990s, and the pressure is now steady rather than episodic.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Very High (63/100), **Rank:** #17 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** 60 to 100 million, registered and unregistered
- **Primary Threats:** Sinicization policy, surveillance, closure of unregistered churches, imprisonment of pastors, ban on religious instruction for minors
- **Legal Status:** Worship legal only in registered venues. Unregistered churches illegal
- **Notable Case:** Wang Yi of Early Rain Covenant Church, nine-year sentence in 2019

COUNTRY: Colombia
TITLE: Colombia — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/colombia
META DESCRIPTION: Colombia is overwhelmingly Christian, and pastors who oppose armed groups and cartels are killed for it. Indigenous converts face expulsion from their own communities.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Colombia, South America, ELN, Cartels, Pastors Killed, Indigenous Christians, WWL Top 50
PERSECUTION TIER: Medium

## Overview

Colombia is over 90 percent Christian, and it remains one of the more dangerous places in the hemisphere to lead a church. Armed groups treat pastors as rivals for community influence. Church leaders who preach against recruitment of minors, drug production, or extortion are threatened, displaced, and killed. A second pattern runs through some indigenous reserves, where converts are punished by their own community authorities. Open Doors ranked Colombia 47th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Armed Groups and the Church

The 2016 peace agreement with the FARC did not end the violence. The ELN, FARC dissident factions, the Clan del Golfo, and Mexican-linked cartels compete for coca, mining, and trafficking corridors in Cauca, Nariño, Norte de Santander, Arauca, and Chocó. Pastors are visible, immobile, and influential, which makes them useful to control and dangerous to ignore. They are killed for refusing to pay extortion, for sheltering young people from recruitment, and for reporting on armed groups.

Rural congregations in contested zones have shut down after leaders fled. Churches have been used as improvised bases and have been caught in fighting.

## Indigenous Communities

Some indigenous reserves operate under legally recognized autonomous jurisdiction. Where community authorities hold traditional religion as a marker of identity, indigenous people who convert to evangelical Christianity have been fined, jailed in community stocks, had homes destroyed, had water and school access cut, and been expelled. Cases have been documented in Cauca and elsewhere. Indigenous autonomy and individual religious freedom collide here, and Colombian courts have ruled in both directions.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** Medium (31/100), **Rank:** #53 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** over 90 percent
- **Primary Threats:** ELN, FARC dissidents, cartels, extortion and killing of pastors, expulsion of indigenous converts
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #47
- **Legal Status:** Full religious freedom in law

COUNTRY: Comoros
TITLE: Comoros — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/comoros
META DESCRIPTION: The Comoros is an Islamic republic where Sunni Islam is the state religion and evangelism is a crime. A few hundred Comorian Christians practice in total secrecy.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Comoros, Indian Ocean, Islam, Converts, Secret Believers, Anti-Conversion Law, WWL Top 50
PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

The Comoros is a small Indian Ocean archipelago of about 850,000 people, over 98 percent Sunni Muslim. The constitution names Sunni Islam as the state religion. Proselytizing any faith other than Islam is a criminal offense. Foreign Christians may worship in a small number of designated places. Comorian citizens who follow Christ cannot worship openly at all. Estimates of indigenous believers run in the hundreds, and they practice in secrecy. Open Doors ranked Comoros 43rd on the 2026 World Watch List.

## Conditions for Believers

A Comorian who converts is dealt with by family and community before the state gets involved. Converts have been beaten, confined at home, divorced, disinherited, and driven off their island. Arrests for proselytism occur, and those detained are pressed to return to Islam. Because the population is small and closely networked, secrecy is difficult to maintain. A believer discovered in one village has few places to go.

The Catholic and Protestant congregations that exist serve foreign residents from Madagascar, France, and elsewhere. They are permitted on the condition that Comorians do not attend.

- **Persecution Score:** High (53/100), **Rank:** #22 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** a few hundred citizens, plus a small expatriate community
- **Primary Threats:** Anti-proselytism law, family and community sanction, small-island exposure
- **Legal Status:** Sunni Islam is the state religion. Evangelism is criminal
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #43

COUNTRY: Democratic Republic of Congo
TITLE: Democratic Republic of Congo — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/democratic-republic-of-congo
META DESCRIPTION: The DRC is overwhelmingly Christian and its eastern provinces are among the deadliest places on earth for Christians, with the ISIS-linked ADF massacring congregations and abducting believers.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: DR Congo, Central Africa, ADF, ISCAP, M23, Massacres, Abductions, WWL Top 50
PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

The Democratic Republic of Congo is around 95 percent Christian and holds one of the largest Catholic populations in the world. It also contains one of the deadliest environments for Christians anywhere. In North Kivu and Ituri the Allied Democratic Forces, which pledged to Islamic State and operate as its Central Africa Province, massacre villagers, burn churches, and abduct believers. Open Doors ranked the DRC 29th on the 2026 World Watch List and places it among the worst countries in the world for abductions of Christians.

## The ADF and Eastern Congo

The ADF began as a Ugandan rebel movement and shifted into a jihadist insurgency after its Islamic State affiliation in 2019. Its method is the night raid on rural villages, killing with machetes and gunfire, burning homes and churches, and taking captives. Massacres have been recorded repeatedly in Beni territory and across Ituri. Worshippers have been killed inside church buildings. A bomb at a Pentecostal church in Kasindi killed at least fourteen people in January 2023.

Ugandan and Congolese forces have run joint operations against the ADF since 2021 without reducing attacks on civilians. The group has also carried out cross-border attacks in Uganda.

## M23 and Wider Collapse

The M23 rebellion, supported by Rwanda according to UN experts, seized large areas of North Kivu and took Goma early in 2025. That offensive displaced enormous numbers of people and broke up parishes and congregations across the province. Well over a hundred armed groups operate in the east. Church institutions run hospitals, schools, and displacement camps and are often the only functioning services in reach.

## The Church's Public Role

The Catholic Church holds unusual political weight here. Its election observation network has challenged official results, and bishops have confronted successive governments over stolen votes and corruption. That role brings pressure and occasional violence, including attacks on churchgoers during protest periods, distinct from the jihadist threat in the east.

## Key Facts

- **Persecution Score:** High (44/100), **Rank:** #33 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 95 percent
- **Primary Threats:** ADF and ISCAP massacres, mass abduction, M23 offensive and displacement, state collapse in the east
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #29, among the highest for abductions
- **Legal Status:** Religious freedom protected in law

Catholics form the largest group, with substantial Protestant and fast-growing revival and Pentecostal churches. Religious freedom is protected and generally respected. Registration is required for religious organizations, and the government has closed churches for failing to meet administrative requirements or for practices judged fraudulent, which affects small revivalist groups more than established denominations. Civil wars in the 1990s destroyed churches and displaced congregations, and church mediation helped end the fighting.

COUNTRY: Côte d'Ivoire (Ivory Coast)
TITLE: Côte d'Ivoire — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/ivory-coast
META DESCRIPTION: Côte d'Ivoire is split between a Muslim north and a Christian south. Religious freedom holds nationally, and jihadist groups from Burkina Faso and Mali press on the northern border.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Cote d Ivoire, West Africa, Islam, Christianity, Sahel Spillover, Jihadism, Basilica
PERSECUTION TIER: Medium

## Overview

Côte d'Ivoire is roughly 40 percent Muslim and 40 percent Christian, with traditional religion alongside both. The north is predominantly Muslim, the south predominantly Christian, and the divide fed the civil wars of 2002 to 2007 and 2010 to 2011 without being their cause. Religious freedom is protected and generally respected. The live threat is jihadist spillover from Burkina Faso and Mali into the northern border zone.

## Background

Catholic and Protestant missions arrived under French rule. Yamoussoukro holds the Basilica of Our Lady of Peace, built under President Houphouët-Boigny and consecrated in 1990, among the largest church buildings in the world. Its scale in a poor country drew criticism at the time and it remains a landmark of African Christianity.

The civil conflicts split the country between a rebel-held north and a government-held south, with religious and ethnic identity used as political shorthand. Reconciliation since 2011 has held, and interfaith relations at the local level are generally workable.

## Jihadist Spillover

Attacks by groups tied to JNIM began in the north in 2020, including a raid on the Kafolo army post. Incidents have continued along the borders with Burkina Faso and Mali. The government has invested heavily in northern security and development to prevent what happened in the Sahel.

COUNTRY: Cuba
TITLE: Cuba — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/cuba
META DESCRIPTION: Cuba's communist government monitors churches, blocks construction, and detains pastors who speak politically. Christianity survived state atheism and is growing through house churches.

PERSECUTION TIER: High

## Overview

Cuba is around 60 percent Christian on paper, and active participation is lower. The Communist Party has controlled religious life since 1959. Direct atheism was abandoned in 1992, and control did not end with it. Churches need permission to build or repair. New congregations struggle to register. Pastors who criticize the government are detained, fined, put under house arrest, or pushed into exile. Open Doors ranked Cuba 24th on the 2026 World Watch List.

## From Atheism to Control

The revolution nationalized Catholic schools, expelled hundreds of priests, and declared Cuba an atheist state in 1976. Believers were barred from Party membership and from many professions. The constitution was amended in 1992 to make the state secular rather than atheist, and Pope John Paul II's visit in 1998 opened further space. Popes Benedict XVI and Francis both visited later.

The opening has limits. The Office of Religious Affairs approves or refuses nearly everything of consequence. Permits to build churches are almost never granted, which is why so much of Cuban Protestant growth happens in homes. The government has demolished buildings it deemed unauthorized and confiscated others.

## Current Pressure

The protests of July 2021 marked a turn. Clergy who supported protesters or sheltered them were detained and interrogated, and several religious leaders were sentenced or left the country. Surveillance of sermons is routine. Independent religious leaders report travel bans, loss of ration access, and pressure on family members.

Economic collapse and blackouts have hollowed out congregations as members emigrate. Church-run humanitarian work has grown into the gap left by failing state services, which the government tolerates and watches.

- **Estimated Christian Population:** about 60 percent nominal, lower in practice
- **Primary Threats:** State surveillance, refusal of building and registration permits, detention of outspoken pastors, demolitions
- **Open Doors WWL 2026:** #24
- **Legal Status:** Secular state since 1992, with religious activity controlled by the Office of Religious Affairs

COUNTRY: Czechia (Czech Republic)
TITLE: Czechia — Christianity & Persecution Profile
SLUG: country-profiles/czech-republic
META DESCRIPTION: Czechia is among the least religious countries in Europe after decades of communist suppression. Christians face no persecution, and the church is small and shrinking.
CATEGORY: Country Profiles
TAGS: Czechia, Czech Republic, Central Europe, Secularization, Communism, Catholic, Hussite
PERSECUTION TIER: Lower

## Overview

Czechia is one of the least religious countries in Europe. The 2021 census recorded under 12 percent identifying with any church, with Roman Catholics the largest group, and roughly half the population declining to answer the question. Religious freedom is complete and there is no persecution.

## How It Got Here

Bohemia produced Jan Hus, burned at Constance in 1415, whose reform movement predated Luther by a century and shaped Czech identity. Catholic re-imposition after the Battle of White Mountain in 1620 tied the church to Habsburg rule in the popular mind, a resentment that never fully cleared.

Communist rule from 1948 to 1989 ran one of the harder anti-religious campaigns in the Eastern bloc. Monasteries were seized in 1950, clergy were imprisoned, and the state licensed every priest. An underground church operated with secretly ordained clergy, including married men. Cardinal Josef Beran was interned for years and died in exile in Rome. A restitution law passed in 2012 returned or compensated for seized church property, which remains politically contested.

Freedom came in 1989 and the church did not recover numerically. Decline has continued. The pressure on Czech Christianity is indifference rather than repression.

- **Persecution Score:** Lower (6/100), **Rank:** #131 of 192 countries
- **Estimated Christian Population:** under 12 percent by the 2021 census
- **Current Status:** Full religious freedom, no persecution
- **Historical Note:** Jan Hus, the 1950 seizure of monasteries, the underground church under communism
